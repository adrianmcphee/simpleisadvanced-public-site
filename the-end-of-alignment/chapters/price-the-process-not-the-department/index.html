Price the Process, Not the Department

A European insurer with five hundred services invests €1.2 million in a shared integration layer for its claims process. The common event infrastructure would let the claims, policy, and payments teams exchange information independently, replacing a fragile web of direct connections that causes an average of three incidents per month.

The platform team delivers a working prototype in four months, and the three consuming teams are willing to migrate, with a projected incident reduction of between 70% and 80%.

The CFO kills the investment in the annual budget review because the platform “doesn't show ROI in year one.” The decision is structurally rational rather than a failure of imagination: he can't approve what the accounting system can't see. The €1.2 million in salaries and infrastructure is visible; the benefits of fewer incidents, faster change, and reduced coordination are diffuse, and none appears as a line item in any business unit's P&L.

Over the following eighteen months, the direct connections cause nine major incidents, two of them customer-facing and one triggering regulatory scrutiny. A claims payment fails because the policy and payments systems disagree on whether a policy is active, a commercial fleet operator waits nine days for a claim that should have settled in two, and his broker emails the CEO. The CTO traces the failure to the integration point the cancelled platform would have replaced, but the incident log has already recorded it as a “data synchronisation issue.” The broker files a complaint with the industry ombudsman. It reaches the compliance function as a request for a root-cause report. The report describes the technical failure accurately and never mentions the investment decision that left the failure mechanism in place, because it follows the incident, not the decision. The CFO who cancelled the platform never sees the complaint: it is handled two levels below him, in a function with no reporting line to the person whose decision left that mechanism in place. The total cost of the nine incidents, including response, remediation, compensation, and regulatory reporting, is approximately €2.1 million, all of it attributed to “engineering quality” in the quarterly review. Nobody connects it to the cancelled platform.

A year later, the budget includes a line item for “integration resilience,” funded at €400,000, one third of the original investment and insufficient to do the work properly. The platform team has been disbanded, and its lead engineer has left.

Finance holds formal authority over capital because its artefacts bind. In the composite, the platform team brought a presentation and a prototype; the CFO brought a budget with sign-off authority. “The numbers don't support it” ended the conversation because the operating costs omitted from the model had no equally authoritative form. Formal artefacts defeat informal arguments through the authority attached to them.

Structural opacity names this condition, in which an organisation's own instruments cannot trace the relationship between its decisions and their systemic consequences.

The pricing error

The benefit of a rejected structural investment accrues to budgets the CFO does not control, while the cost of rejection distributes across departments that cannot trace it to the original decision. In the composite, the costs compound beyond the first eighteen months: in year two, a partial fix is funded at one third of the original investment; in year three, a regulatory audit identifies the integration failures as systemic. The fine is €3 million and the emergency rebuild costs €4 million. Total investment avoided: €1.2 million. Total cost incurred over three years: approximately €9.1 million.

More deeply, the financial model prices the wrong unit of production. Finance prices departments, while value is created through processes. Capital attaches to organisational labels, Customer Engagement, Platform Services, Digital Transformation, which have budgets and headcounts but no correspondence to the mechanisms through which the organisation creates value. The budget records investment in Digital Transformation; the system records changes scattered across services, many addressing symptoms of structural incoherence rather than creating new value. No ordinary financial report captures the capital destruction between those descriptions.

Running the trace yourself

Boards can construct the deferred investment trace before the next meeting in three steps, using existing tooling and no AI or consulting engagement.

Take the three costliest incident categories from the last eighteen months. Use specific recurring failure modes: integration failures between named systems and data consistency errors in a named process, along with manual workarounds for a named capability gap. An aggregate label such as “engineering quality” hides the pattern. Pull the actual costs from finance, grouping response and remediation with compensation and regulatory reporting. Most boards have never seen these costs aggregated across departmental lines, because no reporting line crosses the boundaries the costs cross.

For each category, ask the CTO which structural investment would have prevented or substantially reduced this failure mode. Get a cost estimate; a range is sufficient. Usually, the answer has been in a slide deck, a proposal, or an email thread that was never escalated.

Compare the rejected investment against the attributable incident cost. The ratio is the deferred investment trace. Include only consequences whose failure mechanism the investment would probably have removed, show uncertainty as a range, and test the attribution with someone who did not sponsor the original proposal. The result is a prompt for capital scrutiny, not a universal benchmark.

WORKED EXAMPLE: deferred investment trace Applied to the insurer vignette above, the method yields the following trace for one incident category. Incident category: Integration failures between claims and payments systems through policy data. 18-month incident cost: €2.1 million (incident response, remediation, customer compensation, regulatory reporting across nine major incidents). Structural investment that would have prevented it: Shared event infrastructure. Estimated cost: €1.2 million. Status: rejected in annual budget review. 18-month ratio: 1:1.75. Three-year ratio: Adding the regulatory fine (€3 million) and emergency rebuild (€4 million), the total cost reaches €9.1 million. Ratio: 1:7.6. The ratio does not make the investment case by itself. It exposes costs the original decision did not contain and makes the causal assumptions available for challenge. A CFO who produces the trace for three incident categories has changed the conversation from “the numbers don't support it” to “which costs and failure mechanisms did the numbers omit?”

TSB shows why a visible regulatory number can understate the total consequence. Its disclosures from the 2018 migration put gross post-migration charges at approximately £330 million, while the later combined FCA and PRA fine was £48.65 million. That comparison is not a deferred-investment ratio and proves no counterfactual. It shows that the penalty can be a small part of the operational and customer cost.

The headcount illusion

When an organisation reduces engineering headcount while AI-assisted output holds steady, the natural conclusion is that it has solved a cost problem. It may instead have changed the relationship between production and review. Fewer people can generate more changes while carrying less shared context and less capacity to reconcile them against process intent. The relevant question is whether output rate has risen relative to reconciliation capacity. A board authorising the reduction should inspect that ratio alongside cost.

DORA's surveys illustrate the changing trade-off. Its 2024 report estimated that each 25% increase in AI adoption was associated with a 1.5% reduction in delivery throughput and a 7.2% reduction in delivery stability. Its 2025 report found higher adoption associated with greater throughput and greater instability. The throughput association reversed between the two reports; instability did not. That reversal is not a detail to smooth away. It shows that the relationship is context-dependent and that a survey model is not a stable forecast for any one company. DORA's description of AI as an amplifier is the more durable finding: measure the whole delivery system instead of assuming generated output reaches customers as value.

The coordination conversion

When investment in structural improvement is rejected, the architectural coupling persists and coordination increases. Delivery leads spend their weeks translating among process needs, system constraints, and team commitments. Their work is real, but its demand exists because technical convenience determined the boundaries and left the business process unowned.

Measure how much technology-delivery capacity your company spends on translation, dependency negotiation, status assembly, and approval waiting. Do not begin with a generic benchmark. If the local number is material, the mispriced capital is visible as headcount and has its own supply chain: consulting firms, system integrators, and governance vendors whose revenue benefits when the coordination layer persists. A firm hired to resolve misalignment also needs a recommendation the commissioning executives can absorb. That constraint can select for more alignment and governance layered onto the existing structure, while the harder finding, “no one owns the process,” threatens both executive authority and follow-on work.

Published estimates can show scale but cannot supply the local answer. CISQ's industry model put the 2022 cost of poor software quality in the United States at US\$2.41 trillion, including about US\$1.52 trillion in accumulated technical debt. It is a co-sponsored macroeconomic estimate rather than an observed cost ledger. Use it as a reason to run the trace; the local incident and staffing data must carry the investment case.

The relationship is circular. Capital is rejected because the financial model cannot trace investment to operational cost; coordination increases and delivery slows; scrutiny applies the same model that produced the original rejection. Each iteration adds sediment as coordination roles become permanent headcount, steering committees become governance infrastructure, and consulting engagements become recurring budget lines. An organisation builds institutional commitments around the absence of investment, and each commitment makes the correction more expensive.

The repackaging cycle

Coordination conversion imposes a steady-state cost; the repackaging cycle imposes a periodic and larger one. In a software-dependent corporate, operational work changes how claims are processed, orders are fulfilled, and customers are onboarded. Where process owners hold both engineering capability and decision authority, this change is continuous and small. Where “the business” and technology are separate functions, neither owns enough to make continuous process change. The business does not own the systems, and technology does not own the process. The bridging mechanism becomes a programme large enough to justify a business case, labelled as IT so it fits the budget model, and governed through steering committees. Such programmes carry a recurrent failure mode because the IT label has assigned business change to a function that cannot make business decisions.

What the organisation calls legacy often predates these programmes: systems built by analyst-programmers who once sat inside the business and combined domain knowledge with technical capability, a role discarded when the organisation separated “the business” from technology. People who could have improved those systems incrementally were moved into a function with no authority to change how the process works.

Each failed programme deposits a layer of technical residue: partially migrated databases, integration layers connecting old and new, services running alongside the systems they were meant to replace. Each layer makes the next change harder, which is cited as evidence that the next programme must be bigger. Incumbent vendors reinforce the cycle, protecting recurring revenue from managed-service contracts scoped around the existing complexity, or selling the next large programme, structurally identical to the one that created the legacy.

A board should ask how many programme-scale IT projects the organisation has run in the last decade, how many changed the business process they were created to change, and what residue the unsuccessful ones left behind. If the organisation has run four major programmes and the targeted processes still operate as they did before, the capital produced legacy.

Birmingham City Council provides the documented version of the cycle. Europe's largest local authority replaced its long-standing SAP system with Oracle Fusion against an original business case of £19 million. The external auditor's report records that the steering committee approved go-live in April 2022 without assessing the results of user testing. The new system then left the council unable to produce auditable accounts, while effective fraud controls did not operate across the system's customisations until an anti-fraud module went live some eighteen months later. By early 2026, the council's own forecast for the programme had reached £144.4 million, while an independent academic estimate reached £216.5 million once written-off savings were included. The council had also issued a section 114 notice amid a wider financial crisis to which the ERP failure contributed. Every element of the cycle is in the public record: a representation built to be approved, a governance body that did not test it, costs scattered across budgets that no one traced back to the decision, and a re-implementation larger than the project it replaced.

What AI synthesis reveals

When AI reconciles incident and investment history against the system architecture, it can surface a traceable chain linking cancelled investments to operational costs and coordination headcount through the structural decisions that produced them. The coordination tax remains, now visible enough to investigate and price.

Operational-resilience rules increasingly require firms to demonstrate how critical work and third-party dependencies operate. Structural opacity survives those rules at a higher cost. An organisation that cannot connect its processes, suppliers, systems, controls, and consequences cannot buy that understanding in the quarter a regulator or incident requires it.
