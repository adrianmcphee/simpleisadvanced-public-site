Make the Company Readable

Senior leaders work through representations. The model is necessary; the problem is that its representations can be approved without being tested against the systems they describe.

What a board narrative is

Every board of a software-dependent corporate receives a narrative through its board pack, management reports, and quarterly business reviews. The narrative covers financial and strategic performance, drawing on programme status and risk registers. Each section is produced by a different function and is internally consistent. Directors read finance and technology alongside operations and risk as a composite picture of one organisation, but the sections are parallel descriptions, each in its own language, with its own measures and its own definition of success.

Finance connects margin to cost and revenue but not expenditure to system behaviour. Technology reports activity without proving strategic movement. Operations reports processing and incidents without showing whether the constraint is technical or operational. Each section may be accurate; together they remain unreconciled descriptions of one company.

The useful questions cross those reports. Which process changed for the money spent? What fraction of its elapsed time is waiting for a decision or handoff? Which customer or operating result moved? If no function can answer, the board is reading parallel descriptions of different organisations as one.

Representations that replace understanding

Software-dependent corporates govern through representations: strategy documents, operating models, architecture diagrams, portfolio views, KPI dashboards. These serve a legitimate purpose. No CEO can understand every system in a large organisation, and representations provide the necessary abstraction. The problem begins when a representation replaces the thing it represents. If a portfolio shows “Customer Engagement” as a product with a P&L and a roadmap while the underlying features are scattered across services maintained by different teams, it has confused the label with the system. The label exists; the product does not.

The mechanism of absorption

How does this incoherence survive? The gap between the board narrative and the system is bridged socially, by people whose job is to translate between the two. Roles across programme and delivery management, portfolio analysis, and business partnering are so pervasive that they are invisible. Their work is necessary inside the current structure; without it, the gap between narrative and reality would produce immediate, visible failure. The synthesis they provide remains expensive and fragile because it depends entirely on the continued presence of the people performing it.

The board's constraint

Boards should govern through representations; they cannot understand every service and data flow. The governing question is whether important claims can be tested against the system they describe. Nothing in the conventional model requires that reconciliation, so structural contradictions can be smoothed away before the pack arrives.

Birmingham City Council provides the documented version of the cycle. Europe's largest local authority replaced its long-standing SAP system with Oracle Fusion against an original business case of £19 million. The external auditor's report records that the steering committee approved go-live in April 2022 without assessing the results of user testing. The new system then left the council unable to produce auditable accounts, while effective fraud controls did not operate across the system's customisations until an anti-fraud module went live some eighteen months later. By early 2026, the council's own forecast for the programme had reached £144.4 million, while an independent academic estimate reached £216.5 million once written-off savings were included.

The council's September 2023 section 114 account identified equal-pay liabilities and an in-year budget gap as the immediate financial problem. The University of Sheffield's Audit Reform Lab disputes that causal account and argues that the Oracle failure and continuing service pressures better explain the revenue crisis. The causal disagreement remains contested. The case establishes the representation mechanism: a governance body approved go-live without assessing the evidence it had received, and the replacement grew far beyond the project originally approved.

The writer preserves power; the reader tests it

An AI reader can take a narrow assignment: test whether what exists corresponds to what is claimed. Most organisations deploy the generator first, even when generation is not the binding constraint, because it works inside existing roles and challenges no power.

Used as a reader, AI compares artefacts the organisation previously reconciled socially. Strategy against code is the plainest case: “API-first design” is testable; “drive digital transformation” is not. The reader can cite connections that bypass defined interfaces. Humans still decide what the count means and what must change.

Current tools can already compare a bounded process definition with documents, code, configuration, and operating evidence under human review. Whole-company causal synthesis is not a dependable product. The durable investment is therefore the artefacts and ownership that make a bounded comparison possible; models and vendors remain replaceable.

Cheap, repeatable synthesis changes behaviour when every finding points to its claim and evidence. Continued divergence then becomes a decision with a history. Legitimate limits for privacy, security, legal privilege, and regulatory duty remain. The diagnostic question is whether a limit protects such an interest or merely protects a claim from evidence.

The pattern recalls companies that bolted a polished website onto unchanged fulfilment and service processes. AI copilots and document generators can become the same brochureware: the writer modernises the surface; the reader tests the machinery underneath it.
