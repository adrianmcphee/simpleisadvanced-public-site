Make the Company Readable

The CEO of a mid-sized European insurer funded a three-year technology modernisation programme at €40 million, hired a respected CTO, and approved a new organisational structure. Two years in, with the core measures she cares about unmoved, she commissions an external review, and the consultants produce 120 slides recommending another reorganisation, a new operating model, and a governance framework. She approves the recommendations, because the structure provides nothing else to approve.

The governance model explains the CEO's decision: she works through representations that cannot be reconciled. The model predates software-mediated value creation, and the representations she receives were built for approval without system-level testing.

What a board narrative is

Every board of a software-dependent corporate receives a narrative through its board pack, management reports, and quarterly business reviews. The narrative covers financial and strategic performance, drawing on programme status and risk registers. Each section is produced by a different function and is internally consistent. Directors read finance and technology alongside operations and risk as a composite picture of one organisation, but the sections are parallel descriptions, each in its own language, with its own measures and its own definition of success.

The CFO's section reports margin against budget, with its underlying cost and revenue. It does so accurately, but it does not connect expenditure to system behaviour. In the composite, the €40 million programme appears as a cost line, with nothing to show whether it changed anything in the system that creates value. The CTO's section reports deployment frequency and a new application alongside a stronger recruitment brand, without connecting any of it to whether the organisation can bring a product to market faster. The COO's section reports processing times and incident counts without explaining whether performance is constrained by the system or by the people running it. Each section is locally consistent; taken together they are globally incoherent, because the organisation built no mechanism to reconcile these representations against the system that implements them.

What the board pack says, what it omits, and what to ask Finance reports cost, revenue, and margin against budget.\ Omits: whether expenditure changed anything in the system that creates value.\ Ask: for the money spent on this programme, which process metrics moved? Technology reports deployment frequency, uptime, and recruitment.\ Omits: whether engineering activity connects to strategic outcomes.\ Ask: which strategic priority can now be delivered that could not be delivered last quarter? Operations reports processing times, SLAs, and incident counts.\ Omits: whether performance is constrained by the system or by the people running it.\ Ask: for the slowest process, what fraction of elapsed process time is spent waiting for a decision or handoff rather than doing work? If no function can answer its question, the board is governing three parallel descriptions of different organisations and reading them as one.

Representations that replace understanding

Software-dependent corporates govern through representations: strategy documents, operating models, architecture diagrams, portfolio views, KPI dashboards. These serve a legitimate purpose. No CEO can understand every system in a large organisation, and representations provide the necessary abstraction. The problem begins when a representation replaces the thing it represents. If a portfolio shows “Customer Engagement” as a product with a P&L and a roadmap while the underlying features are scattered across services maintained by different teams, it has confused the label with the system. The label exists; the product does not.

The mechanism of absorption

How does this incoherence survive? The gap between the board narrative and the system is bridged socially, by people whose job is to translate between the two. Roles across programme and delivery management, portfolio analysis, and business partnering are so pervasive that they are invisible. Their work is necessary inside the current structure; without it, the gap between narrative and reality would produce immediate, visible failure. The synthesis they provide remains expensive and fragile because it depends entirely on the continued presence of the people performing it.

The board's constraint

Boards were designed to govern through representations, and that is appropriate. A board cannot and should not understand every service and data flow in a large organisation. The governing question is what happens when the representations cannot be tested against the system they describe. When they are accurate, governance works. When they are disconnected from reality, a board can approve a major programme without knowing that the system cannot deliver the business case without structural changes the programme omits. The board is governing through artefacts that reach it only after the structural contradictions have been smoothed away. This constraint is structural. Nothing in the current model requires any representation to be testable against the system, so the board keeps approving descriptions that no one is obliged to reconcile.

The prologue contains the opening case: a system reads claims and mechanisms together, the technical leader recognises the findings, and the company suppresses the result because its board narrative is different. The mechanism matters more than another version of the story. AI makes previously scattered contradictions cheap to assemble, and assembled contradictions create a governance choice.

The reader comes first

AI's significance for organisations lies in reading artefacts repeatedly without inherently privileging hierarchy or narrative confidence. Its assigned task can be narrow: test whether what exists corresponds to what is claimed. Most organisations use it in the wrong order. An AI generator accelerates a visible part of the system even when code generation was not the binding constraint. Design remains hard because someone must decide what to build, where responsibilities lie, and how the pieces interact. Those decisions depend on clear business processes, explicit ownership, and precise descriptions of how value is created, which in many software-dependent corporates do not exist in a form any tool can reason about. An AI generator also reinforces the comfortable assumption that AI works for people inside the existing structure, threatening no role and challenging no power structure. The reader challenges both roles and power, which helps explain why organisations tend to deploy the generator first and neglect the reader.

Used as a reader, AI takes artefacts designed to exist independently and reads them together, surfacing contradictions the organisation has been maintaining socially. In the prologue, the strategy, architecture model, service catalogue, repositories, deployment records, and telemetry already existed; the missing act was to compare them. Strategy against code is the plainest case. “API-first design” is testable; “drive digital transformation” is not. A reader can enumerate connections that bypass defined interfaces and cite the relevant paths. Humans still decide what the count means, whether an exception is justified, and what must change.

The reader exposes labour that was never visible. Humans bridge gaps among strategy, process, software, and reporting with context, intuition, and social negotiation. They absorb contradictions and carry exceptions in their heads. When authoritative descriptions are absent, both humans and models fill the gaps by inference. Human gap-filling disappears into institutional knowledge. AI gap-filling arrives as an output that can be inspected and challenged.

The response may be to constrain the reader: limit access to sanitised artefacts, review findings before circulation, or filter synthesis through the narrative layer it was meant to test. Some limits are necessary for privacy, security, legal privilege, and regulatory duty. The diagnostic question is whether the constraint protects a legitimate interest or protects a claim from evidence.

Why cheap synthesis changes behaviour

Suppression is rational and unstable. It can persist while reconciliation costs more than maintaining separate narratives. Cheap synthesis changes that ratio. Its economic gain is repeatability with provenance: the same bounded comparison can be run again against changed artefacts, and every finding can point back to the claim and mechanism that produced it. Reduced human effort may follow, but it is not the essential gain. A one-off report can be absorbed. A repeatable comparison turns continued divergence into a decision with a history.

For boards, cheap synthesis creates a new choice: demand representations that can be reconciled, or continue governing through claims no one is required to test. Chapter 14 returns to the operating mechanics, controls, and limits of a continuous reader. The point here is the change in institutional economics. The company no longer needs a heroic individual or a bespoke review merely to discover that its descriptions disagree.

The pattern recalls 1999. Many companies bolted a polished website onto unchanged fulfilment, inventory, and service processes. The interface looked modern while the business remained untouched. AI copilots, chatbots, document generators, and meeting summaries can become the same brochureware. The writer path modernises the surface. The reader path tests the machinery underneath it. Most organisations buy the surface first because it leaves the operating structure intact.
