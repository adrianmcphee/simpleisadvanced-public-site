What to Do on Monday

Elena opens the renewals reconciliation before the quarterly review. One finding is red: the strategy update says automated risk reassessment is deployed, while the process definition, source, deployment manifests, and production events show modified policies still entering a manual queue.

She does not begin by asking who wrote the update. She asks the process owner whether the comparison is sound. The unit lead brings the platform lead and two people from renewals operations into the review. They trace the finding from the customer event through the production route. The automation is absent. A vendor milestone was signed off, copied into the programme report, and carried into the board pack as an operating fact.

Elena withdraws the sentence. The minutes record the discrepancy, the evidence, and the period during which the unsupported claim appeared. The CEO asks the renewals owner for an outcome milestone, not a revised status narrative. The owner proposes one complete path for the highest-volume policy type, with the manual team defining the exception rules, one delivery lane, controlled exposure, and a measured comparison against the current queue. Finance attaches the existing programme cost to the renewals process so the next decision can be made against outcome rather than department.

Nobody announces a transformation. Nobody opens an alignment workstream. The company corrects its record, gives the discrepancy an owner, and turns it into one testable movement through production.

The technical event belongs to the same class as the one in the prologue: a claim meets the running system and fails. The institutional event is the opposite. The report circulates. The board tests it. Authority follows the evidence.

For years, that reconciliation lived in someone's head. In many organisations, someone carries the real system. That person knows which diagrams are fiction, which products have no operable boundary, and which processes survive because somebody maintains their exceptions personally. A private notebook can be closer to the running company than its official artefacts.

The notebook can now become a reconciliation report. For one defined process, the synthesis previously performed socially across years of accumulated context can be repeated as infrastructure, with domain review, at a cost low enough to use operationally. It does not yet run against every artefact continuously; that integrated capability remains custom engineering. The expert has not been replaced. Their judgement has become inspectable and repeatable.

Each quarter that the organisation operates AI as a narrative tool builds institutional sediment around the writer path. Roles, budgets, and governance rhythms calibrate to the output. The switching cost compounds with every governance cycle that reinforces it. Meanwhile, the external environment moves in the opposite direction: a regulator, an acquirer, or a litigant can commission its own synthesis. Internal suppression no longer controls external discovery. The preconditions cannot be bought in the quarter they turn out to be needed.

Autonomous agents compound the urgency. An agent deployed without process ownership, explicit boundaries, and versioned contracts encounters the same structural incoherence that exhausted the humans whose social reconciliation it was meant to replace, except that its failures arrive faster and cannot be smoothed over through interpretation. A board that authorises agent deployment without the structural preconditions is investing in machine-speed reproduction of the dysfunction described throughout this book.

The governance condition

Once reconciliation is technically possible, the absence of reconciliation becomes a governance fact. The question is why the organisation chose to leave its own representations unverified.

The redistribution of ownership, authority, and accountability cannot safely be delegated in full to a transformation programme whose sponsors are affected by the result. A technology team can build the machinery but lacks the authority to redesign the corporation. A consultancy can support the diagnosis and transition, but its engagement incentives do not transfer the governing decision. The choice belongs to the people with authority to redistribute power: the CEO and the board.

Once the board has seen a reconciled synthesis, ignorance is no longer available in the same form. The Post Office Horizon Inquiry shows the retrospective cost of unreadability: years spent assembling records and testimony to reconstruct what happened, what information existed, and how authority was exercised. As of August 2026, Volume 1 of its final report covered human impact and redress; the remainder of the findings was still being prepared. The lesson here does not depend on anticipating those findings. A decision that ignores reconciled evidence leaves a timestamp in the board minute, the committee record, and the quarter in which no action followed.

Elena picks up the reconciliation report. The notebook that once lived in someone's head is now open on the table. Whether the board acts on it or not, the record will show that it could have.

Begin with one process

The scale of the diagnosis tempts a grand response. Resist it. Do not announce an enterprise transformation, appoint a central programme, or buy a platform before the company can define what the platform is meant to make true. Those moves recreate the structure this book is trying to remove.

On Monday, choose one material process. Choose one close enough to customer value that its failures matter, bounded enough to understand, and painful enough that capable people already know where the contradictions are. Refunds, renewals, claims triage, supplier onboarding, account recovery, fulfilment, pricing, or release management can all work. The name matters less than the ability to identify its initiating event and completed outcome.

Within thirty days, require seven things.

First, state the customer or operator promise. Make it specific enough to disappoint somebody if it is not met.

Second, write the process as it actually operates: states, transitions, decisions, data, exceptions, manual work, suppliers, agents, controls, and failure behaviour. Preserve the aspirational version separately. Do not combine the two and call the result a target operating model.

Third, name one accountable leader and give that person authority over the process mechanism: operations, software, operational data, contracts, deployment, capability composition, first-line risk, cost, and outcome. Record corporate constraints and escalation thresholds. Remove ordinary approval rights that contradict the accountability.

Fourth, map the source, modules, operational state, deployables, feature flags, provider contracts, and production evidence that implement the process. Where the map cannot be completed, record the gap as a finding rather than smoothing it into a diagram.

Fifth, reconcile one material claim with the running system. Use AI to read, compare, and cite. Have the domain owner judge the meaning. Give every discrepancy a disposition and a named authority.

Sixth, select one milestone and deliver one complete vertical slice through a named lane with exact leases, a joined seam test, trunk integration, controlled exposure, production verification, and mechanical retirement of temporary state.

Seventh, price the process. Connect its people, software, suppliers, failure cost, coordination overhead, and capital to an observable customer and economic result. Stop letting the cost disappear across departments while the outcome belongs to nobody.

At the end of the month, the company will possess more than a pilot. It will possess a test of its operating model. Can an accountable unit describe reality without permission? Can it change the process without reconstructing the whole company in meetings? Can governance challenge it without becoming shadow leadership? Can finance see the cost and outcome together? Can an AI reader reproduce a useful comparison from authoritative artefacts? Can the temporary delivery machinery retire cleanly?

The answers will expose what the corporate structure currently protects. That is useful. The first unit earns its value by revealing a boundary, veto, shared state, reporting fiction, or capability decision the existing model kept implicit; it need not represent the whole company.

Eight years after his first operating-model engagement, Felix leads a small assignment for another financial-services company. This time the client has already used AI to reconcile its strategy, process material, repositories, and production evidence. It does not need thirty-one interviews and a deck explaining that ownership is fragmented. The contradiction is in the evidence pack.

Felix's first draft recommends one process, one accountable unit, a baseline, a charter, and a complete delivery slice. It explicitly rejects an alignment office. The engagement partner marks the paragraph and asks where the follow-on governance programme has gone.

“If the unit works, they should not need one,” Felix says.

The partner reads the reconciliation again. A fluent diagnosis is now cheap, and the client will not pay to have its own evidence translated into 120 slides. The valuable work is harder: drawing a boundary, moving authority, defining proof, and staying long enough to see whether the unit performs. She leaves Felix's paragraph in the proposal.

Felix has not become heroic. The market around his judgement has changed. Precision no longer weakens the engagement; drawing the boundary and staying for the result are the parts the client cannot generate for itself. The adjustment he learned at twenty-six has become the weaker commercial answer.

The optimistic case

The brutal diagnosis should not be confused with pessimism. Software-dependent corporates contain an extraordinary accumulation of domain knowledge, customer relationships, regulated permissions, operational experience, capital, and working software. Their problem is rarely an absence of intelligence. It is that intelligence has been divided by a design that separates authority from mechanism and then pays large coordination layers to bridge the gap.

That design can change.

The replacement does not require waiting for general artificial intelligence, a perfect data estate, a clean technical architecture, or a generation of executives to retire. It requires one process to become explicit, one leader to receive real authority, one unit to join the capabilities the outcome needs, one technical path to ship through controlled temporary authority, and one recurring reconciliation between promise and reality. Each successful unit makes the next easier because contracts, evidence, and institutional confidence accumulate.

AI improves the economics at exactly the point large organisations have been weakest. It can read more of the company than any committee, preserve provenance across more artefacts than any heroic individual, and repeat the comparison often enough for Drift to become an operational signal rather than a periodic revelation. Humans retain the decisions that deserve human authority: what should be promised, which trade-offs are acceptable, what evidence means, who bears risk, and when the company should change course.

The companies that make this move will not merely code faster. They will learn faster. Their architecture will describe real authority. Their products will correspond to operable systems. Their governance will receive evidence it can test. Their people will spend less of their intelligence translating between structures that should never have been separated. Their agents will act inside boundaries precise enough to be useful and safe.

Most important, the people closest to reality will stop being organisational irritants and become the mechanism of progress. Their precision will no longer be an interpersonal problem to manage. It will be an economic advantage the company can compound.

The window is open because synthesis has become cheap while the knowledge required to judge it still exists. It will not stay open forever. Start with one process, make one promise real, and let the running company become more authoritative than the story told about it.

Reality is no longer optional. That is good news.
