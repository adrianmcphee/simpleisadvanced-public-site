The Alignment-Industrial Complex

The enemy is a system, not a profession. Consultants, programme leaders, architects, product managers, Scrum Masters, and executives can all do intelligent and necessary work inside it. The problem is the equilibrium their work can sustain: a company keeps authority fragmented, capable people compensate through alignment, the compensation makes the structure survivable, and its survival creates demand for more compensation.

Composite

Felix is twenty-six and eighteen months into his first job at a management consultancy. He studied computer science and economics at Delft.

He is staffed on a technology operating model engagement at a mid-sized European financial services company. The brief is familiar: technology investment isn't producing proportional outcomes, the CEO suspects something structural but can't name it, and the board has approved a six-week diagnostic.

Felix conducts thirty-one interviews in three weeks, mapping dependencies through stakeholder perception rather than code inspection. His picture is accurate in its essentials, with ownership fragmented and strategy detached from system behaviour, yet entirely disconnected from the machinery it describes.

Felix presents his findings to the engagement partner on a Friday afternoon. The partner is experienced, intelligent, and not unkind. She reads the deck closely. “Fragmented authority” becomes “unclear accountabilities.” The proposed process-owning unit moves to an appendix. In its place she writes “cross-functional governance model” and circles it once.

The recommendation can't suggest that the organisational structure is the problem, because the executives who approved the engagement are the organisational structure. It can't propose collapsing coordination roles, because the people in those roles are the engagement's primary stakeholders. It can't recommend placing engineers inside process-owning business units, because “the business” doesn't recognise engineering as part of itself, and the recommendation must be legible to those who will approve it.

What the recommendation can propose is better alignment: a new operating model with clearer accountabilities, a governance framework connecting strategy to delivery, and alignment rituals that produce the visibility the board is asking for. The final page offers an implementation phase to put the recommendations into practice. It is the most carefully costed page in the deck.

The partner has learned, over a decade, that an acceptable recommendation travels further than a correct diagnosis the client will reject. Her approach is rational inside constraints she did not create. The deliverable can now be approved, implemented, and extended. Felix's original recommendation would have removed some of the work required to implement it.

The Alignment-Industrial Complex is an equilibrium rather than a conspiracy. Each participant behaves rationally within their constraints: the partner keeps the recommendation acceptable and the work extendable, the client executive minimises exposure, and the governance framework produces visible activity no one needs to defend as a lie.

The board approves the follow-on engagement unanimously. Within a week, the new operating model has become a headcount negotiation: three executives compete for the alignment roles, each proposing new hires and reporting lines, and a fourth invokes regulatory oversight to add review gates, attestations, and a RACI matrix requiring three workshops to finalise. Each cites the consultancy's recommendation; none mentions a customer journey, a business process, or a system.

To the board, the activity looks like leadership engagement, when the operating model meant to resolve fragmented ownership has become the mechanism through which ownership fragments further. The remedy is being metabolised by the disease.

Five months later, the implementation programme begins by recommending a refresh of the original diagnostic, because the organisation has changed: it now has more committees.

Felix moves to his next engagement. He doesn't mention this to anyone.

The next client is a logistics company in Hamburg. Different industry, different city, same pattern: fragmented ownership, an operating model from a previous consultancy, the same gap between diagnostic and deliverable. The partner adjusts the recommendation in the same way, for the same reasons, and this time Felix doesn't object. The recognition no longer bothers him. He has become fluent in the adjustment, a capable person who concluded that precision wasn't worth what it cost him, and stopped insisting on it.

Fragmented ownership has been institutionalised and monetised. Roles, frameworks, incentives, and vendors that began as coping mechanisms now make deferral stable without requiring a conspiracy. “We need better alignment” acknowledges disappointing outcomes without examining the design that produced them.

When conditions change, people compare views and decide. That coordination should end when the new understanding has changed the governing artefact, process, or contract. When it becomes a permanent product, each new boundary creates more of it and each reorganisation creates translation. The work cannot finish until ownership closes the distance between decision and consequence.

Alignment meetings are the machinery

A recurring alignment meeting is often the visible machinery of missing ownership. Several functions bring partial information; nobody owns the complete process; the chair can manage the discussion but cannot decide. Minutes record conversation without changing the process definition, software, data, contract, or control. Recurrence then looks like evidence of the chair's importance rather than evidence that the boundary remains broken.

The cost is much larger than the hour in the calendar. There is the delay until the meeting, the preparation of a version each function can defend, the private conversations before it, the translation during it, the approval sought afterwards, and the next meeting required when an absent stakeholder objects. One material decision can consume weeks while appearing in management data as several well-attended hours. The meeting destroys speed through latency and accountability through diffusion.

AI compresses the time required to read evidence, compare artefacts, and implement a bounded change. It does not compress a fortnightly steering cycle. As production accelerates, standing coordination becomes a larger share of elapsed time and strategy.

A temporary meeting may still be necessary for an incident, new cross-unit seam, or decision that genuinely crosses authorities. Before it ends, name the owner, record the ruling in the governing artefact, change the process or contract, and state when the coordination ends. To test a recurring forum, stop routing one class of ordinary decision through it while preserving legal and incident escalation. Stalled decisions reveal missing authority, conflicting actions reveal a missing contract, and lost information reveals a missing artefact. If nothing material breaks, the forum may be maintaining a ritual rather than an operating dependency.

Agility is strategy

If strategy explains how a company intends to win, agility is the speed and fidelity with which valid evidence becomes changed operating behaviour. The loop begins with a customer, market, control, or operating signal and ends only when the responsible unit has changed the process, exposed the change safely, observed the result, and corrected course. Shipping code is one step inside it.

Many companies changed the ceremony without changing that loop. Plans became sprints and status meetings became stand-ups while commercial context, process authority, data, control, and release rights remained elsewhere. Scrum can coordinate local work; it cannot supply missing authority. OKRs can express intent; they cannot give a team control of the mechanism expected to move the number.

Real agility therefore requires a unit that owns a complete process and outcome, plus the capability to change it inside explicit constraints. It needs a baseline, a hypothesis, guardrails, and evidence from operation. How the unit organises its daily work is its decision. A company can become exceptionally efficient at completing sprints while drifting away from its strategy.

Why the problem survives

Senior leadership rarely sees the structural cause because each layer absorbs contradiction before it travels upward. The problem reaches the board framed as delivery, talent, or market performance. Structural correction is harder to approve because it redistributes authority, while “better alignment” acknowledges disappointing outcomes without naming whose authority must change.

AI procurement can preserve the same equilibrium: a coding assistant gives an unaccountable team faster output while a governance tool gives the coordination layer richer artefacts. Visible adoption rises without shortening the evidence-to-outcome loop. The more disruptive use of AI is to test whether authoritative claims match the running system closely enough to act.

Why this is not another framework

A framework can survive because its vocabulary is elastic. A workshop can succeed, a maturity score can improve, and a new role can be installed while the running company remains unchanged. The objects in this book earn no such protection. A process definition is wrong when production follows rules it omits. A contract is wrong when the parties cannot test its promise. A drift report is wrong when its claims cannot be traced to the behaviour it compared.

Those objects do not require a branded method, a certification, or a prescribed ceremony. A company may represent them in plain text, code, a governed registry, or another form its operators will maintain. The requirement is that each object has an owner, change history, consequence, and way for operation to prove it wrong.

The model can still be sold badly. Consultants can turn process-owning units into a target operating model, vendors can call a dashboard continuous reconciliation, and a central office can certify compliance while retaining every decision right. The test is whether authority moved, whether the artefacts constrain behaviour, and whether production evidence can defeat the represented company. If not, the framework industry has absorbed another criticism and returned it as merchandise.
