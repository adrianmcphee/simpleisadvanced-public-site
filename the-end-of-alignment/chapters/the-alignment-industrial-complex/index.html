The Alignment-Industrial Complex

The enemy is a system, not a profession. Consultants, programme leaders, architects, product managers, Scrum Masters, and executives can all do intelligent and necessary work inside it. The problem is the equilibrium their work can sustain: a company keeps authority fragmented, capable people compensate through alignment, the compensation makes the structure survivable, and its survival creates demand for more compensation.

Composite

Felix is twenty-six and eighteen months into his first job at a management consultancy. He studied computer science and economics at Delft.

He is staffed on a technology operating model engagement at a mid-sized European financial services company. The brief is familiar: technology investment isn't producing proportional outcomes, the CEO suspects something structural but can't name it, and the board has approved a six-week diagnostic.

Felix conducts thirty-one interviews in three weeks, mapping dependencies through stakeholder perception rather than code inspection. His picture is accurate in its essentials, with ownership fragmented and strategy detached from system behaviour, yet entirely disconnected from the machinery it describes.

Felix presents his findings to the engagement partner on a Friday afternoon. The partner is experienced, intelligent, and not unkind. She agrees with the diagnosis, then explains which parts of it can appear in the final deliverable.

The recommendation can't suggest that the organisational structure is the problem, because the executives who approved the engagement are the organisational structure. It can't propose collapsing coordination roles, because the people in those roles are the engagement's primary stakeholders. It can't recommend placing engineers inside process-owning business units, because “the business” doesn't recognise engineering as part of itself, and the recommendation must be legible to those who will approve it.

What the recommendation can propose is better alignment: a new operating model with clearer accountabilities, a governance framework connecting strategy to delivery, and alignment rituals that produce the visibility the board is asking for. It also recommends a follow-on engagement to implement the model, usually the most expensive line item in the proposal.

The partner has learned, over a decade, that achievable improvement is more valuable than the correct diagnosis the client will reject. Her approach is rational: she is optimising within constraints she didn't create. The primary revenue generator is the follow-on engagement, while a structural fix of the kind Felix initially proposed would reduce the demand for continuing alignment support and therefore the revenue attached to it.

The Alignment-Industrial Complex is an equilibrium rather than a conspiracy. Each participant behaves rationally within their constraints: the partner maximises revenue, the client executive minimises exposure, and the governance framework produces visible activity no one needs to defend as a lie.

The board approves the follow-on engagement unanimously. Within a week, the new operating model has become a headcount negotiation: three executives compete for the alignment roles, each proposing new hires and reporting lines, and a fourth invokes regulatory oversight to add review gates, attestations, and a RACI matrix requiring three workshops to finalise. Each cites the consultancy's recommendation; none mentions a customer journey, a business process, or a system.

To the board, the activity looks like leadership engagement, when the operating model meant to resolve fragmented ownership has become the mechanism through which ownership fragments further. The remedy is being metabolised by the disease.

Five months later, the implementation programme begins by recommending a refresh of the original diagnostic, because the organisation has changed: it now has more committees.

Felix moves to his next engagement. He doesn't mention this to anyone.

The next client is a logistics company in Hamburg. Different industry, different city, same pattern: fragmented ownership, an operating model from a previous consultancy, the same gap between diagnostic and deliverable. The partner adjusts the recommendation in the same way, for the same reasons, and this time Felix doesn't object. The recognition no longer bothers him. He has become fluent in the adjustment, a capable person who concluded that precision wasn't worth what it cost him, and stopped insisting on it.

Fragmented ownership has not survived by accident. It has been institutionalised and monetised.

What began as coping mechanisms for complexity inside software-dependent corporates has evolved into a self-preserving system of roles, frameworks, incentives, and vendors whose demand persists while structural incoherence persists. No participant needs to intend permanent deferral; together they can make deferral the stable outcome.

The Complex converts structural incoherence into billable work. When organisations fail to produce value proportional to their technical investment, the diagnosis often becomes “we need better alignment” and leaves the organisation's design unexamined.

Alignment becomes the default answer because it is the safest answer: it acknowledges the failure without threatening the structure that produced it.

In a healthy organisation, alignment is transient. It appears when conditions change and dissipates once shared understanding is restored. In a software-dependent corporate, alignment becomes a standalone product: what organisations do when they refuse to assign ownership. Every new team boundary creates demand for coordination; every reorganisation, for translation; every failure, for retrospectives. That work never completes, because completion would require collapsing the distance between decision and consequence, which would eliminate the need for alignment itself.

Alignment meetings are the machinery

A recurring alignment meeting is often the visible machinery of missing ownership. Several functions bring partial information. Nobody owns the complete process. The chair can manage the discussion but cannot decide. A disagreement becomes an action to socialise the issue elsewhere. Minutes record that a conversation occurred without changing the process definition, software, data, contract, or control. The same people return the following week because nothing in the structure made their return unnecessary.

The role can become a corporate talk-show host: convene the factions, invite each side to tell its story, perform empathy and firmness, restate the common purpose, and announce alignment. The audience is required to attend, and the performance is presented as leadership. Because the structure guarantees another episode, recurrence can become evidence of the role's importance rather than evidence that the boundary remains broken. Repair ownership, contracts, and decision rights, and much of the stage disappears. The system rewards capable executives for managing a conflict it keeps recreating.

The cost is much larger than the hour in the calendar. There is the delay until the meeting, the preparation of a version each function can defend, the private conversations before it, the translation during it, the approval sought afterwards, and the next meeting required when an absent stakeholder objects. One material decision can consume weeks while appearing in management data as several well-attended hours. The meeting destroys speed through latency and accountability through diffusion.

AI makes that arrangement newly untenable. It compresses the time required to read evidence, compare artefacts, explore options, produce analysis, and implement a bounded change. It does not compress a fortnightly steering cycle. As production becomes faster, standing coordination becomes a larger share of elapsed time and therefore a larger part of strategy. A competitor with process authority inside an autonomous unit can observe, decide, change, expose, and learn while the aligned company is still finding a date when every stakeholder can attend.

The rule is simple. A temporary meeting may be necessary for a new cross-unit seam, an incident, or a strategic decision that genuinely crosses authorities. Before it ends, name the decision owner, record the ruling in the governing artefact, change the relevant process or contract, and state when the temporary coordination ends. If the meeting must recur to keep ordinary work moving, do not improve the meeting. Repair the boundary.

One bounded test is to stop routing a class of ordinary decisions through its coordinator while preserving incident and legal escalation. Observe what breaks. A stalled decision identifies missing authority. Conflicting action identifies a missing contract. Lost information identifies an artefact that should exist independently of the person. If nothing material breaks, the recurring coordination may be maintaining a ritual rather than an operating dependency. The test diagnoses the structure; it is not a judgement on the individual who has been compensating for it.

Consulting firms are hired to diagnose misalignment and must produce a cure the client can absorb. The constraint selects for frameworks that manage the problem while preserving its structural cause. Procurement performs the parallel move for technology programmes: an unresolved business problem becomes a purchasable platform, an integrator, a statement of work, and governance. The form makes ambiguity look contracted. It cannot create the business definition or authority the contract presupposes.

Agility is strategy

If strategy explains how a company intends to win, agility is part of strategy. A company that can turn valid evidence into changed operating behaviour faster than its competitors can test more serious bets, falsify bad ones earlier, lose less money while learning, and respond while an opportunity still exists. Speed is not merely an execution concern after strategy has been decided. The ability to learn and act at speed changes which strategies are possible.

The strategically relevant cycle begins when a customer, market, control, or operating signal changes. The unit compares that evidence with a known baseline and an intended outcome, decides what it means, changes the process and the software, data, or control that implements it, exposes the change safely, observes the result, and corrects course. Strategic agility is the elapsed time and fidelity of that complete evidence-to-outcome loop. Shipping code is one step inside it.

Why Agile is fake in many companies

As a corporate operating-model movement, Agile failed. Some of its local engineering practices remain useful. The promised structural agility largely did not appear. Annual plans became quarterly plans; requirements were divided into sprints; status meetings became stand-ups; project managers acquired new titles; work moved across electronic boards. The organisation still kept commercial and process context on one side of a boundary and treated engineers as delivery capacity on the other. Budgets still funded projects. Data remained fragmented. Architecture and control functions still held approval queues. Release authority still sat outside the team. Decisions still travelled through the same hierarchy. The work became iterative while the company remained inert.

The failure eventually announced itself in the movement's own language. Scrum Masters complained that “business people” would not leave “the team” alone or let it decide. The complaint sometimes described real interference, but it was also a confession: the team had been designed outside the business context and now treated separation from that context as autonomy. Commercial commitments, operating exceptions, customer evidence, process economics, risk, and control lived elsewhere. Asking such a team to decide without that expertise or accountability was not empowerment. It was isolation with a sprint cadence.

“The business decides what; technology decides how” preserves the same division in a friendlier sentence. The important decisions do not divide cleanly that way. What is commercially valuable depends on what the system and process make possible. How a process works changes its cost, risk, customer experience, and strategic options. A group should have local implementation authority, but the answer to missing authority is to redraw the unit until the relevant context and decision rights sit together. It is not to demand that a technology team be protected from the people who understand the operation and customer.

The opposite slogan, “the team decides,” is equally empty. Which team decides what, with whose capital, against which customer promise, within whose risk appetite, and who carries the consequence? A collection of people does not acquire unlimited authority because a framework calls it a team. Authority belongs to a defined unit that owns a complete process and outcome, operates under a charter, contains the relevant expertise, and has an accountable leader. Decision rights inside that boundary can be broad and delegated. They still need a subject, a scope, and an owner.

Scrum is not the structural cause. It is a local coordination method that can be useful to a unit that already owns an outcome. It cannot supply customer contact, process authority, data rights, an operating baseline, or the ability to release a change. A stand-up cannot join contexts the organisation has deliberately separated. A retrospective cannot repair a budget model that rewards project completion. A person called Product Owner who carries requirements from “the business” to a technology team is a courier, whatever the framework says the role should be.

The same failure recurs in many OKR programmes. Leaders write objectives before establishing what is happening now. Measures lack a stable definition, provenance, or observation window. Functional hierarchies cascade related language into separate goals, then spend a quarter negotiating whether their numbers add up. Engineering receives a roadmap as its key result while commercial, operations, and risk keep the context that produced it. This is the old split expressed numerically. An objective without a baseline, a named process, a causal hypothesis, a trustworthy signal, and an owner able to alter the mechanism is an aspiration with a reporting obligation.

What real agility requires

Real agility begins with an autonomous business unit. A software team alone lacks the necessary authority. The unit owns a complete process and outcome, contains the expertise needed to operate it, and can change the process, software, data, controls, and exposure path inside explicit policy and agreed contracts. Chapter 6 defines those rights in full. The evidence-to-outcome loop belongs to one accountable structure.

The unit needs a measurement system before it claims to be agile. It establishes a baseline with volumes, distributions, variation, cohorts, seasonality, provenance, and known gaps. It distinguishes lagging outcomes from indicators that reveal movement early enough to intervene, then adds guardrails so that one improving signal cannot quietly damage another outcome, customer group, control, or risk position.

Leading indicators are hypotheses, not miniature outcomes. If a claims unit raises straight-through processing while complaints and reopened cases rise, it has accelerated the wrong behaviour. The unit must be close enough to see the divergence, trace it through the process and system, and change the mechanism. That joined capability makes a short feedback cycle strategically meaningful.

Without an autonomous unit, a baseline, leading indicators, lagging outcomes, and guardrails, Agile has no object to make agile. It can measure how quickly work moves through a technology team, but not whether the company is learning or improving. Velocity, deployment frequency, and cycle time may diagnose the delivery system. They cannot establish that the right process changed, the intended customer outcome occurred, or the economics improved. A company can become exceptionally efficient at completing sprints while drifting away from its strategy.

How the unit coordinates its work is its own decision. It may use Scrum, Kanban, pairing, a daily conversation, or almost no ceremony. Keep a practice when it shortens the evidence-to-outcome loop or improves its fidelity. Remove it when it becomes a substitute for either.

AI makes this distinction unforgiving. In a divided organisation, agents increase the volume of specifications, code, analysis, and reports entering queues whose underlying decisions remain slow. Output rises while strategic agility does not. In an integrated unit, the same tools let people read the complete context, test a hypothesis, change the running process, and reconcile the result sooner. The competitor that places engineers inside the business process will learn faster than the company that keeps trying to align them with it. A company that classifies engineering, data, AI, and cybersecurity as concerns outside “the business” has defined the capabilities through which it operates outside the business. No methodology can rescue that contradiction.

Fragmented ownership slows the learning loop. The Alignment-Industrial Complex supplies the roles and rituals through which the delay becomes permanent. The answer is not a better ritual. It is a structure in which evidence can reach the people with authority, and those people can change the mechanism before the opportunity passes.

Why CEOs rarely see the problem

Senior leadership rarely sees the structural cause because each layer absorbs contradiction before it reaches them. Dashboards lag reality, reports summarise interpretation, and status is curated before it travels upward, so by the time a problem reaches the board it has been framed as a delivery, talent, or market issue. Few roles are rewarded for surfacing the contradiction early. External advisers may face a parallel constraint when the engagement must produce a recommendation the commissioning executives can absorb and fund.

Some leaders are genuinely insulated by the reporting system. Others see the problem and rationally defer a disruptive correction whose benefit is delayed. A third group derives authority from brokering the fragmentation. The responses differ, but the governing fact is the same: structural correction redistributes authority. Chapter 13 returns to the people and roles that lose scope when the company becomes legible.

The Alignment-Industrial Complex has absorbed the arrival of AI tooling. Felix's pattern applies to AI procurement. An AI coding assistant sold to a team that does not own its process produces faster output without clearer ownership, and an AI governance tool sold to a coordination layer gives that layer richer artefacts to coordinate around. The purchase can satisfy board expectations while leaving the coordination layer intact, especially when procurement evaluates visible adoption rather than a shorter evidence-to-outcome loop.

Alignment as the last safe language

Alignment survives because it is the last safe language. It allows leaders to acknowledge problems without naming causes, organisations to appear responsive without redistributing power, failure to be discussed indefinitely without assigning responsibility, and everyone involved to remain rational within a dysfunctional structure.

AI threatens this equilibrium by making coherence inspectable and reducing the need to negotiate it socially. It makes a previously expensive assumption testable: can the organisation reconcile its authoritative artefacts against the running system with enough provenance to act? Having embedded alignment into their administrative machinery through finance, HR, budgeting, and hiring, many organisations will resist that test until the cost of pretence exceeds the cost of change.
