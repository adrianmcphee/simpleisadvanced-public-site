Make Coordination Structural

A badly designed contract can encode the wrong boundary as efficiently as a meeting. If a payments unit requires every consumer to submit fully validated account data, origination may push checks into the customer journey while claims adds retries around cases its process cannot verify. The provider tightens rejection rules; consumers build workarounds; coordination overhead moves into code.

The correction is structural. Each unit validates the data it owns, while the payments unit publishes a check for payment-specific fields. The contract becomes useful only after accountability follows the information and decisions on each side of the boundary.

Coordination between teams is often managed through committees: steering groups, dependency boards, change advisory boards. They exist because no explicit mechanism governs how one team's output becomes another team's input, so the interaction is negotiated socially and repeated whenever conditions change. This chapter applies the structural correction in three places: units interact through contracts, data leaves a unit as a published output, and central services earn demand from units that own their risk. Each move replaces recurring social negotiation with an explicit boundary.

What a contract is, and what it replaces

A contract is the operational equivalent of a supplier agreement with versioned terms. It specifies how one unit interacts with another and records its promises, dependencies, and change behaviour. A social agreement depends on memory and goodwill; versioned tests make violations detectable without continuous human interpretation. A contract front-loads design and must be maintained as the interaction changes, but it makes each subsequent exchange testable against the artefact rather than a person's recollection. New interactions still require design, and failures still require investigation.

For a board member unfamiliar with software versioning, the question when terms change is the same as in any supplier relationship: are changes announced within clear boundaries with compatibility preserved, or do they arrive without warning and break existing arrangements? When the payments unit modifies its API, it publishes a new version. Old and new coexist for a defined period, consumers migrate on their own schedules, and an AI reader can flag contracts approaching end of support and identify consumers that have not migrated.

What violation looks like

The value of a contract depends on what happens when it is violated. In a committee-based model, Team A may discover that Team B's output no longer matches expectations only after something breaks in production. An incident bridge then debates responsibility and negotiates the fix. In a contract-based model, the expected output is explicit, so divergence can be detected and attributed to a specific interaction. The two parties can resolve it against a shared artefact without reconstructing the promise on a bridge call.

On a Tuesday at 10:14, the claims unit's monitoring flags a contract violation: the payments settlement interface has begun returning timestamps in a different format from the contract. The claims lead sends her counterpart the clause and examples of the divergent output. A library upgrade changed the default serialisation. The payments unit corrects it by 11:40. The shared artefact made the failure attributable, and two people resolved it without a bridge call.

Why contracts replace committees

Committees are expensive even when necessary. A committee meeting costs the time of every participant and produces a social agreement that begins degrading when conditions change. A contract costs more to establish and must be maintained, but its terms persist across subsequent interactions. For a board, the intended difference is visible in two metrics: coordination cost, the proportion of technology staff absorbed by translation, and change velocity, the time from decision to deployment. A working contract should reduce the first and improve the second. Disagreement remains; a contract can encode wrong assumptions as easily as right ones, but the failure is visible and resolvable through a contract change without a blame negotiation. Contracts require ownership. A contract between two units only works when each owns a coherent process and can decide about it independently. Without ownership, contracts become another layer of documentation that no one maintains; with ownership, they become the mechanism through which the organisation coordinates without committees.

Publish data as outputs

A data analyst in the central reporting team at a Nordic insurer spent three weeks building the quarterly capital allocation report for the CFO. It required revenue-per-customer figures across Motor, Property, and Commercial; he pulled the numbers from each division's data warehouse, and they didn't reconcile. Motor counted a customer as a policyholder, Property as a household, and Commercial as a legal entity with an active contract in the trailing twelve months. A corporate client with a fleet policy, a building policy, and a liability policy might therefore appear as three customers, one, or zero depending on renewal dates.

He spent four days in meetings with divisional data owners trying to agree a common definition. They couldn't, because each definition was correct within its own process. He built a reconciliation layer in a spreadsheet that mapped between the three using manual rules, with an “assumptions” tab that ran to forty rows. The report shipped two weeks late, and the CFO used it to allocate €12 million in discretionary capital across the three divisions. The numbers he compared were built on three incompatible definitions of the unit they measured. Nobody told him, and nobody needed to: the report looked precise because it inherited the precision of the source systems, which agreed on the number of decimal places but not on what they were counting.

The CFO allocated €12 million on numbers whose reconciliation existed only in one analyst's spreadsheet. For the board, the implication is specific: a shared operational data model can become a shared point of failure. Cross-process reporting is legitimate; a common operational substrate is the wrong mechanism when it couples every writer and reader to every change. The “single source of truth” then becomes a single point of coupling. Data should leave autonomous units through published contracts, separate from their operational stores.

Two kinds of data

Data is at least two structurally different things, and conflating them is a recurring source of data-governance failure. Operational data lives inside the unit and serves the process it owns; each unit models the entities it works with in the way that serves its process, and the apparent duplication preserves precision. Analytical data is the cross-cutting view the organisation needs for reporting and decision-making, where data from multiple units is brought together. Data contracts bridge the two without a shared operational database. Each unit publishes analytical outputs through a contract and the reporting layer consumes them. When the unit changes its internal structures, it continues to honour the contract; when the contract itself must change, the change is versioned and communicated as an API version change would be.

Other units do not treat internal operational tables as an integration interface; governed diagnostic and audit access remain possible. Consumers and reporting pipelines use declared outputs. The mechanism is analogous to financial reporting, where each business unit produces its own management accounts and the group consolidation function combines them according to defined rules, with no unit reaching directly into another's ledger.

The test of autonomy

The autonomy test is whether a unit can change its internal data model and publish a backward-compatible contract version without a meeting.

A claims unit adding a fraud-risk score to its analytical output should be able to publish a compatible version with the field optional, retain the previous version for a declared migration period, and let consumers move on their own schedules. If a data architecture group must first collect impact assessments and grant approval, the contract has not created local authority.

If a change like this requires a steering group, calling the arrangement data contracts disguises centralised governance. Centralised models can optimise for reporting coherence at the expense of change velocity; distributed ownership can optimise for change velocity; distribution with contracts seeks to balance both, at the cost of maintaining the contracts.

What the board should understand

Process-owned units and published contracts provide the accountable producers from which a data architecture can be built. They do not make cross-process semantics effortless; the group still needs explicit mappings, quality rules, lineage, and consolidation logic. If the organisation tries to fix data architecture without fixing ownership, however, it produces another governance layer over definitions that nobody has authority to maintain.

In the composite, the analyst eventually works differently. Each division publishes its customer count through a data contract: Motor policyholders, Property households, and Commercial legal entities with active contracts. The definitions do not need to become identical; each remains correct inside its own process. The mapping between them is published alongside the data as a versioned specification of how the three reconcile to the group reporting definition. The forty-row assumptions tab becomes a maintained contract, tested automatically before the quarterly report is assembled. The next allocation is built on numbers whose reconciliation logic is explicit, inspectable, and owned.

Data governance begins with ownership. A definition needs an authority that can maintain it, and a cross-process view needs explicit reconciliation among those authorities. Fixing the data alone does not hold when the ownership underneath remains ambiguous.

Make central services earn demand

The first quarterly review after the transitional fund renewal included the CISO's report on security posture across the four operational units: 94% compliance with the mandated container scanning policy.

Thomas, one of the non-executive directors, asked a question the metric couldn't answer: “What does 94% compliance mean for our actual security risk?” The CISO explained that the scanning tool was integrated into every unit's deployment pipeline. He described the remaining 6% as a configuration issue being resolved. Thomas asked a different question: “Are the units using the tool because they need it, or because we told them to?”

The answer took two weeks. Two of the four units had configured the tool to auto-approve all findings below critical severity, because the default thresholds generated hundreds of false positives and the units had no mechanism to work with the security team on tuning them. The dashboard showed adoption while the security risk was unchanged. “We are measuring whether the units consume what we push,” Thomas observed, “not whether they are managing the risk they own.”

The central security team had two responses available. One was to retool: work with each unit to tune the thresholds to its context, and offer the tool as a service the units would choose to consume. The other was to reframe: present the 94% as evidence of successful adoption, add a remediation plan for the 6%, and keep pushing the unchanged tool. The CISO chose the second. Six months later the dashboard showed 100%. Thomas asked whether the false-positive rate had changed; the CISO couldn't say, because the dashboard measured adoption, not substance. The units had learned to comply without consuming.

Overt opposition is one reversal mechanism; a quieter one is accretion. Central functions push capabilities while ignoring demand, and governance obligations accumulate one reasonable request at a time until the charter's authority has been hollowed out without anyone deciding to hollow it.

Push versus pull

The default relationship between a central function and the rest of the organisation is push; the central function defines what is needed, builds or procures it, and mandates its adoption. Security tools are mandated, compliance frameworks imposed, observability standards prescribed. Push appears responsible because it produces universal coverage metrics a board can review. It can also produce compliance without substance: pushed observability can create dashboards nobody uses for operational decisions, pushed compliance can create checkbox adherence, and pushed standards can create superficial conformity. Units adopt on paper, second-line oversight verifies the appearance of compliance while missing the substance, and risk ownership exists in the dashboard without operating in practice.

How accountability creates demand

The autonomous unit model changes the incentive. Each unit owns its process and the risk attached to it, is monitored against its contracts and published SLOs, and is audited against those commitments. That accountability creates genuine demand for capabilities the unit cannot efficiently build itself. A unit that owns its process risk needs security capabilities it cannot build in-house; a unit that publishes SLOs needs observability that serves its own operations. Each unit pulls for these because its accountability requires them, and adoption is genuine because it solves a problem the unit owns. The demand pattern itself becomes board-level evidence. Each unit's quarterly report shows which central capabilities it consumed and why. If three of four units consume the central security tool and one built its own, asking why is diagnostic: the central tool may be weak, the unit's context may genuinely differ, or its governance may have drifted.

Three conditions the board should verify

The demand model applies to service capabilities. Independent audit, statutory duties, and second-line challenge exist because the institution requires them. They should never disguise a mandatory control as voluntary demand, or use a mandatory role to force unrelated tools and services.

For a central service, the demand model works only when three conditions hold. First, the capability must be genuinely good; a security tool that generates hundreds of false positives gets configured to pass compliance while leaving risk unchanged. Second, it must be affordable; if consuming a central service costs more than building locally, the units have a rational argument for fragmentation. Third, its service budget should reflect aggregate unit demand rather than a mandate that guarantees survival regardless of quality.

The board's test

The annual test for a central service is simple: if it had to earn funding through unit demand, would the units choose it? If yes, it is probably providing value at a rational price. If no, test whether a genuine institutional obligation justifies it or whether mandate is protecting a weak service. A board should ask every year because recentralisation occurs by accretion: a security gate here, a data requirement there, an architectural approval above a threshold. Track the resulting unit time and outcome. Name the relationship honestly as service, governance, or shadow leadership.
