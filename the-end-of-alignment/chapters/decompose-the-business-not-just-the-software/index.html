Decompose the Business, Not Just the Software

A logistics company adds a new delivery status, “held at customs,” to customer tracking. Where the customs and tracking units own their state and interact through a versioned contract, the provider can publish the status and the consumer can render it on its own release schedule. Where both read a shared database, the same change can require approval from every affected service owner before work begins.

The delay exposes the cost of a structural decision: the system was split without splitting the problem. A board sees a change-velocity problem whose cause is ownership. The shared database is the structural equivalent of a ledger that five departments report from: any modification to its structure requires the consent of every reader, whether or not the modification affects them.

Microservices were often adopted to enable independent deployment inside an organisational structure that could not otherwise move: split the system into many smaller pieces, each deployed on its own schedule, and teams would move faster while changes stayed contained. Consider a bus. It carries everyone using one driver, one maintenance schedule, one parking space. Replace it with twenty separate cars and you feel autonomous until you notice the six tow ropes bolted to your chassis that permanently couple you to every car around you. The split followed vehicle type while ignoring destination, so no car carries everything it needs to complete a journey alone, and you end up with the maintenance cost of a fleet and the manoeuvrability of a bus. Splitting the code let teams ship without coordinating releases while leaving business processes fragmented across units; the cars were separated, but the convoy remained intact.

Architecture that cannot bind

What matters is the distinction between advisory and binding architecture. Advisory architecture uses reference models, capability maps, and target-state diagrams to describe what should exist, and can be updated without touching the system. Binding architecture embeds decisions in code, interfaces, and data contracts, so that an engineer knows, without asking anyone, what she can change independently and what requires coordination. In large software-dependent corporates, architecture is too often primarily advisory; the system evolves according to its own logic, the artefacts according to theirs, and the two diverge.

The board test before a technology reorganisation Before approving a technology reorganisation, ask for three things: the business processes affected, the shared state that crosses the proposed boundaries, and the number of changes in the previous quarter that required coordination across them. Without those three, the board is approving an org chart without knowing the architecture it will produce.

What the board sees

Deployments are distributed without ownership following them. True autonomy requires that a unit own a business process end to end, that the data it produces is owned locally and accessed only through published contracts, and that some duplication is tolerated. A single operational data model can improve reporting coherence while coupling every writer and reader to its rate of change. Distributed ownership improves local change velocity but requires explicit analytical contracts to recover a coherent group view. Organisations that choose the shared model without acknowledging that trade-off produce simulated autonomy: teams deploy independently but cannot evolve independently. That is false autonomy.

A trivial rule change taking months exposes an ownership problem. When no unit owns the process end to end, incidents cascade across boundaries that were supposed to be independent, and the incident bridge becomes the only place the process is reconstructed. Every strategic change negotiates the same dependency structure, so the board expects delivery in one planning period, the work consumes several, and the board concludes that engineering is slow. Coupling constrains delivery. For regulated critical processes, organisations may also be required to demonstrate end-to-end understanding; a process spread across many services and dependencies may be understood by no one as a whole. The DevOps Research and Assessment (DORA) programme's 2019 report measured the delivery gap across its survey categories: elite performers deployed 208 times more frequently than low performers, with lead times 106 times faster. Microservices fail as an independence mechanism when the organisation decomposes services without first decomposing business processes, defining ownership, and establishing contracts. The architecture on the whiteboard follows the process map; the architecture in production follows the org chart.

The correspondence between the org chart and the architecture has a research literature behind it. A Microsoft study of Windows Vista found that metrics describing the organisation, including how many engineers touched a component, how many reporting lines they spanned, and how often ownership changed, outperformed the code metrics tested in that study when predicting failures. A separate Harvard study comparing commercial and open-source codebases found evidence that a product's structure mirrors the communication structure of the organisation that builds it. On this evidence, an org chart functions as an architectural decision. A board that approves a reorganisation changes the conditions under which its systems will evolve, whether or not it intends to.

Products that do not exist in the system

The product review is held monthly, each product owner presenting to the CPO and a panel of senior stakeholders. The owner of “Customer Engagement” presents third: a funnel, a set of KPIs, a roadmap colour-coded by quarter, fluent and confident. Customer Engagement is growing, retention is improving, and the team delivered twelve features this quarter.

Then an enterprise architect asks, “Which services comprise Customer Engagement?” The owner begins to answer, then pauses. Customer Engagement isn't a system but a label applied to features spanning four services maintained by three teams, two of which report into a different product area. The notification service is shared with Operations, the recommendation engine sits inside a legacy monolith nominally owned by Transactions, and the analytics pipeline runs on infrastructure maintained by a platform team that was recently reorganised.

The question requires a forty-five-minute sidebar, in which a delivery lead draws boxes on a whiteboard that no one will photograph, and by the end no one in the room, including the owner, can draw a coherent boundary around Customer Engagement. It has a P&L, a headcount allocation, and a roadmap; it doesn't have a boundary that corresponds to anything in the system. The review continues. Nobody suggests the product definition might be the problem, and the action item is for the owner to “work with architecture to clarify the technical landscape.” It will be deprioritised within two weeks, which is itself a form of clarification.

The architect's question is diagnostic, and many software-dependent corporates have a version of this problem.

What a fake product is

A fake product is a P&L label with no executable boundary. It has an owner, funding, a roadmap, quarterly reviews, and KPIs, yet lacks a boundary corresponding to anything in the system that creates and delivers value. It was defined by market narrative or portfolio taxonomy, displacing the coherent business process that could have been owned, executed against and measured. When reality refused to conform, the organisation avoided revisiting the definition by inserting a translation layer: a product owner tasked with making the incoherent appear coherent.

The product role in the middle

The role is not the error. The boundary is. Many product managers and product owners have real customer knowledge, commercial judgement, domain depth, and a better view of the whole than anybody around them. The structure spends those capabilities on mediation.

The confession appears in a familiar job description: the product manager is the interface between “the business” and engineering. That sentence accepts the separation and hires a person to make it survivable. Intent travels towards engineers as requirements, priorities, and backlog items. Constraints travel back as estimates, dependencies, and reasons the commitment will not fit. Reassurance travels upwards as a roadmap. The person in the middle is expected to preserve coherence among groups that do not share context or authority.

The assignment becomes impossible when the supposed product is a portfolio label. It may consume all of one team, half of another, and occasional capacity from two more. Its notification logic belongs to one service owner, its customer state to another, and its commercial metric to a reporting system none of them controls. The composition changes with each initiative. The product manager has responsibility for a noun on a slide and negotiating rights over the mechanisms underneath it.

This produces two predictable failures. First, product management becomes a courier function. A product owner may order a team's backlog while lacking authority over the process, operational data, architecture, suppliers, release, or economic outcome. Calling that person an owner makes the absence of ownership harder to see. Second, the role becomes a proxy accountability. When the label misses an outcome, the product manager is answerable; when a dependency must change, the decision belongs somewhere else. Engineers experience arbitrary demands from “product.” Product managers experience engineering as resistant. Both are responding rationally to a role designed around a missing boundary.

AI does not repair this arrangement. It can draft more requirements, restate customer interviews, maintain a larger backlog, and produce a more fluent roadmap. None of that grants authority or creates a product in the system. Agentic delivery makes the defect more expensive because the translation layer can now generate work faster than the organisation can integrate the meaning.

How to recognise a fake product

Boards can identify fake products without looking at code; the symptoms are visible in governance artefacts.

The boundary question fails. When “which services comprise this product?” requires a forty-five-minute sidebar, the product's boundary is not operationally legible. The architect's governance question asks whether the thing being funded and measured corresponds to the thing being built.

Features ship while outcomes stay still. Twelve features are delivered and retention ostensibly improves, yet the customer experience is unchanged because the features are scattered across systems that share no coherent process and do not compose into anything the customer experiences as a product.

Dependencies are permanent. The product cannot change without coordinating with teams in different product areas. Every change requires negotiation and every release requires synchronisation. The ownership exists only on paper.

The P&L is synthetic. Accounting convention allocates revenue and cost without tracing either through systems the product owns. The board has approved a reporting label. Its precision reflects the allocation method while obscuring the value-creation mechanism.

Incident responsibility is diffuse. When the product breaks, the bridge call needs people from three or four teams, because no single unit owns the end-to-end process. An owner is “accountable” without authority over the systems involved, producing the performance of governance without the ability to govern.

Why fake products persist

Fake products persist because they serve a governance need. They give the board something to fund, measure, and hold accountable, they fit portfolio frameworks, and they make the organisation legible at the level boards and investors require. Dissolving one would mean admitting that the business process underneath the label is the real unit of value creation, and that admission pulls at once on the product owner's role, the CPO's portfolio, and the board's oversight. The product remains legible at the reporting layer and unverifiable at the system layer.

For boards, this is a capital allocation problem. A board that approves material capital for “Customer Engagement” believes it is investing in a capability. If Customer Engagement has no coherent boundary, the money is distributed across services maintained by different teams, filtered through dependency negotiations, and consumed by coordination. The investment produces activity without compounding into capability because the unit of investment differs from the unit of production. Financial reports may not surface this because the portfolio taxonomy obscures how the system is structured. Once AI synthesis is available, an approval attached to a label with no boundary becomes discoverable in ways it was not before.

A two-engineer counterexample

Product judgement is real and necessary. It does not require a product-management department or a person with that title. Product authority owns a customer promise, the product vision, its economics, the quality bar, and the ordered outcome milestones. It works directly with the engineers, operators, designers, and domain experts who own the processes through which the promise becomes real. Engineers participate in customer and commercial context; whoever holds product authority participates in mechanism and evidence. Neither sends work across a border called “the business.”

I recently helped launch a startup that has moved rapidly with two engineers, no product managers, and no plan to hire one. The operating arrangement is instructive as a possibility proof, not as evidence of prevalence or causation.

The engineers work in the business. Together we examine business processes and customer flows, watch the data, talk about what customers are doing, choose the next consequential change, ship it, and learn. Nobody converts a commercial conversation into requirements for an engineering recipient. Nobody owns a backlog on behalf of people excluded from the customer context. Product judgement is present everywhere it is needed. It is exercised jointly by the people who understand the customer, the economics, the process, and the system.

That arrangement was designed before it was accelerated. I spent time with the founders analysing the business processes, making the important states and decisions explicit, then decomposing the system around those processes. Each bounded context received a ubiquitous language shared by the founders and engineers and used directly in the code. Within a context, the operational term, the decision rule, the event, and the software name are the same words. Where two contexts mean different things, the contract names the translation once at the boundary instead of requiring a person to perform it in every conversation.

The operating result is unusually simple: nobody has to translate a founder's description into language an engineer can use. The engineer already uses the language in the model, tests, events, and production evidence. A changed business rule can be discussed against the state transition it affects and observed in the customer flow after release. The up-front work did not attempt to specify the product forever. The processes, boundaries, and language were strong starting hypotheses that continuous iteration and data could correct. Precision made learning faster because the team could see exactly which assumption changed.

It also creates unusually effective AI use. The model does not have to infer which of several political vocabularies is authoritative before it can help. It reads the process, domain language, code, tests, customer flow, and operating data through the same lens the humans use. It can analyse a discrepancy, propose or implement a bounded change, and help inspect the result without generating a fresh translation layer. The humans retain product and process judgement; AI multiplies execution and reading inside a context whose meaning is already explicit.

There is no meaningful internal politics over which description should prevail. Decisions, code, and operating evidence are visible to everyone involved, and nothing important is protected inside a function's private narrative. A small founder-led company has an advantage here that a large corporation cannot copy by declaration. The lesson is to preserve the conditions as it grows: shared facts, explicit authority, common language inside each context, and contracts where contexts meet. Otherwise growth manufactures the hidden information and interpretive territory that a product-management layer is later hired to navigate.

Two engineers at a startup do not settle the design of a corporation with ten thousand people, regulated processes, and a portfolio of products. The arrangement is enough to challenge the assumption that a product-management interface is intrinsic to serious product development. As the company grows, it can preserve the same joined context inside autonomous units, or it can split commercial and operational judgement from engineering and hire a third column to carry information across the gap. The second choice will feel like maturity. It is the moment the company begins paying people to survive a design problem it has just created.

A product need not correspond to one repository, one service, one deployable, or even one autonomous unit. It must correspond to an inspectable operational composition. Its promises map to owned processes; those processes map to source, state, deployables, contracts, exposure, and outcome evidence. Where several units contribute, the product authority orders the outcome milestones and the units interact through explicit contracts. It does not invent a permanent matrix of partial team allocations and call the resulting negotiation ownership.

This yields three honest categories. A portfolio label is a useful market or reporting view and claims no operational autonomy. A product authority owns a promise, vision, economics, and milestone sequence. A process-owning unit owns the mechanism, data, delivery, first-line risk, and result. One person may carry more than one of those authorities in a small product. In a large platform they may be distributed. The relationships remain explicit.

A person with a product-manager title can become the product authority, process owner, or domain expert inside such a unit. The valuable capabilities move with them: customer understanding, judgement, prioritisation, synthesis, and care for the promise. What survives is the authority, not necessarily the job family. What disappears is the requirement to translate between engineers and a business context from which the engineers were excluded. Product judgement becomes part of the business that operates the product instead of a third column placed beside it.

Before continuing, consider the largest product in your portfolio. What customer promise does it own? Which processes fulfil that promise? Can an engineer trace those processes through source, state, deployables, contracts, and exposure? Who can change each part? If the answer is half of one team, all of another, and negotiated time from a third, who owns the complete result? If you cannot answer those questions, ask the CTO and product authority to map them together in the room at the next board meeting. The answer may reveal a real product, a useful portfolio label, or an invented boundary. The inability to answer is itself the finding.

The operating model therefore begins where the startup did: with a customer promise and an explicit process, not a role chart.
