Start with the Promise

Mortgage origination makes the problem concrete. A digital team may describe the customer journey from form to funds, credit risk may describe an auditable decisioning flow, and operations may describe document checks, exception queues, and disbursement. Each view can be internally coherent while none owns the transitions between them.

A workshop can connect those views in a diagram, but the diagram remains descriptive unless it is versioned with the process and reconciled when the application, decisioning, or fulfilment system changes. Otherwise a data-format change creates a manual workaround, the delay is attributed to operations, and the agreed diagram survives untouched.

One of the most persistent sources of confusion in software-dependent corporates is the belief that business processes and customer journeys are different concerns, owned by different functions and reconciled only when a problem makes the disconnection visible.

A customer journey is an outside-in view of how value is experienced, the sequence of interactions through which a person achieves something meaningful: apply for a mortgage, file a claim, renew a policy, return a product. It emphasises the intended outcome and any friction along the way.

A business process is an inside-out view of how that same value is produced, the activities and decisions that drive required state transitions: receive an application, check completeness, assess creditworthiness, generate an offer, verify documents, disburse funds. It emphasises responsibility for execution within constraints, including the failure modes.

A customer journey and a business process describe the same mechanism observed from opposite sides. The seams become visible wherever responsibility, state, or information passes from one owner to another.

Between the journey and the process sits the customer promise, the specific commitment the organisation has made and must fulfil. The journey is how the customer experiences the promise; the process is how the organisation delivers it. Where the promise itself is not explicit, both become unstable, because the customer experiences a commitment the organisation cannot name and the system is asked to implement a behaviour the business has not defined.

If the mortgage journey and the origination process are not reconciled, the applicant feels one reality while the organisation thinks it delivered another.

In organisations that treat the journey as primary, customer journeys become aspirational artefacts: narratives stripped of operational detail that constrain nothing, much as a landscape painting is admired because it makes no claim about how to get anywhere. They live in decks prepared by design teams or strategy consultants, shown at offsites and taped to walls.

Business processes, where they exist at all, become internal plumbing, documented perfunctorily for compliance or audit. They live in process registers maintained by quality teams. They describe the organisation's regulatory obligations while omitting what it does for customers.

When journeys are vague, engineers build against undefined success criteria. When processes are implicit, no one owns the transitions where work fails. When neither is reconciled with the other, the organisation reasons in disconnected fragments, each locally coherent, none aligned, no shared artefact to bind them.

The disconnect between journeys and processes is why so many initiatives fail in ways no one can explain after the fact: each artefact looks reasonable in isolation, but no one can say how they were meant to work together. When the customer experiences failure (a delayed application, a lost claim, a renewal with the wrong premium), the organisation records poor execution and conceals the structural incoherence. The customer blames the company. The company blames a team. The team blames the process, which was never written down.

For those who must operate within implicit processes, every question about what happens produces “it depends”: on which version, which upstream system, which format. Who owns the outcome if something goes wrong? “We all do,” which means nobody does. The answers remain social, inferred from history and negotiated in real time, and change becomes risky because it threatens assumptions nobody can enumerate. The organisation is held together by the accumulated context of its longest-serving employees, which leaves when they do.

Formally documenting a process is an act of structural commitment, and the resistance it provokes often comes from people who have been holding the process together. They fear, with good reason, that formalising it will expose its fragility and bring blame despite years of compensating for it. Explicitness can feel punitive to the very people who have been compensating the hardest, yet leaving the process dependent on continued presence and goodwill creates a different kind of fragility.

The antidote to implicit, presence-dependent processes is to insist that customer journeys and business processes are views of the same value-producing mechanism, and that both must be precise enough to constrain each other. This raises the question of what “precise enough” means, and what it costs to find out. Many organisations have never been required to answer it.

Write reality down so it bites back

Lisa, a new senior engineer, spends her first week reading. The team lead has been thorough: a shared drive with architecture diagrams, a Confluence space with process descriptions, a wiki with onboarding guides. There is an operating model document, updated seven months ago, that describes the end-to-end contract lifecycle, from origination through maintenance and renewal, in careful detail.

By her third day, she has questions. The operating model describes an automated validation step for contract amendments, run before a change is applied to a live agreement, that the system she has access to doesn't contain. A comment in the code, dated two years ago, reads “validation bypassed pending new pricing rules,” and those rules were never written. The validation exists in the documentation and in the quarterly metrics report, which lists amendment validation rates as a KPI; it doesn't exist in production.

Lisa asks the team lead, who isn't surprised. “That document describes what we were building towards,” he says. “The system describes what we actually did.” He opens a Slack channel and scrolls to a thread from eighteen months ago. “This is where the real decisions are. Most of them, anyway.”

Over the next two months, she will learn what the system does, one incident at a time. She will also understand why nobody updated the documentation: doing so would mean admitting how far the system has drifted from what was approved, at a cost nobody has been willing to pay.

Some documents are structurally prohibited from being true. They exist in abundance across software-dependent corporates, creating the appearance of clarity without constraining behaviour.

The crucial distinction is between writing something down and committing to it. An artefact only matters if it removes options, collapses ambiguity, and forces consequences.

In organisations where reality is optional, documents are designed to do the opposite. They preserve flexibility, defer hard decisions, and can be agreed without changing how anything works.

Every reader has met the document that is sixty pages long, was reviewed by eleven people, took three months to produce, and constrains nothing. Its purpose is to be referenceable, not to be true, like a safety certificate hanging in a building that has never been inspected.

Structural explicitness is an act of exposure: to write a process down in sufficient detail is to declare what happens, who is responsible, and what failure looks like, and to state strategy in executable terms is to accept that it can be tested and found wanting. Explicitness can provoke resistance because it strips away plausible deniability.

A process description that merely names phases, “Application,” “Assessment,” “Approval,” “Fulfilment,” is harmless. It looks structured, sounds deliberate, and says nothing. It does not tell you what happens when an application is incomplete, who is responsible for assessment, or how long it should take, and it cannot be tested, because it asserts nothing.

By contrast, a process description that specifies states, transitions, invariants, and failure modes is dangerous. It can be interrogated. It can be contradicted by reality. It can be used to assign responsibility. It says: “When an application enters the Assessment state, it must have a completed credit check and verified identity documents. Assessment must produce an Approved or Declined outcome within 3 business days. If no outcome is produced within 5 business days, the application is escalated to a team lead.” This creates genuine accountability. It can be tested, and it can fail. At full resolution it becomes a definition precise enough to bite back: every state named, every failure mode explicit, every contract dependency traced.

Similarly, a customer journey drawn as a linear story is inspirational. It shows a happy path, with smiling stick figures and satisfying arrows. It can be presented to a board and framed in a corridor.

A customer journey expressed as a sequence of interactions with defined entry and exit conditions is binding. It demands that the system behave accordingly or admit that it does not. It says: “The user submits an application. The system acknowledges receipt within 2 seconds. The user receives a provisional decision within 24 hours. If the decision requires additional documents, the user is notified with a specific list of items.” When the system does not behave this way, the discrepancy is visible, measurable, and attributable.

The cost of failing to reconcile intent with deployment has a documented consequence. Over the last week of July 2012, Knight Capital deployed new trading code to seven of its eight production servers; on 1 August the eighth silently ran a dormant routine the deployment was meant to retire. In forty-five minutes the firm sent millions of unintended orders into the market, producing more than four million executions, and realised a pre-tax loss of approximately US\$440 million, recorded in its own securities filings and in the SEC order that followed. Knight had no written procedure requiring a second technician to review the deployment, and nobody recognised that the eighth server held different code. The first effective reconciliation was performed by the market, at full price. Binding artefacts and deployment checks aim to force that comparison before the consequence instead of by it.

For engineers working against documents that constrain nothing, the result is constant rework: assumptions are violated, dependencies appear without warning, and when they ask “Where is this defined?” the answer is “It was discussed.” Discussion is a social event; definition is a structural commitment.

The confrontational quality of explicitness is structural feedback, not cruelty. Organisations that use written definitions to constrain themselves can replace recurring argument with specific correction. Fewer assumptions remain hidden, boundaries become easier to inspect, and coordination has a concrete object.

Existence alone doesn't make a business implementable. Its promises, processes, terms, data, and ownership must be explicit enough for a system to enact them without heroic interpretation; until then, software implements assumptions about the business rather than the business itself.

The question is whether the organisation can tolerate what happens when it writes reality down: when the artefacts stop being documents and start being mirrors.

Explicit, binding artefacts have always been available. AI raises the consequence of their absence; the need predates it.

Back in the composite, Lisa starts writing reality down anyway. There is no ticket for it and no one has asked. Her plain text file describes what actually happens between submission and settlement, including states the wiki omits, a retry absorbing a data-quality problem upstream, and a manual check retained after an old incident. She updates it when the system changes, which is how she knows the official process description has not changed in two years. She is not sure what the file is for. It is the only description that would survive contact with the event logs, and it has one reader.
