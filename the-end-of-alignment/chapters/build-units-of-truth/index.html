Build Units of Truth

The payments unit consists of nine people: two senior engineers, three engineers, a domain expert who spent twelve years in payments operations before joining, a data analyst, a reliability engineer, and a unit lead who was previously a senior architect.

Together they own the entire payments process: premium collection, claims disbursement, broker commission payments, and refunds, along with the services that implement those flows, the data those services produce, and the contracts through which other units interact with it.

On a Wednesday morning, the domain expert notices that the refund failure rate has crept from 0.3% to 1.1% over the past month. Rather than file a ticket or schedule a meeting, she checks the payment provider's recent release notes, finds that a response format changed three weeks ago, and suspects the refund service is still expecting the old structure. She walks to the engineer who maintains it: “Refund failures are climbing. The provider changed their response format on the fourteenth. I think our error handling still expects the old one.”

The engineer checks the change history and confirms she is right. The fix is twenty lines of code, deployed after lunch, and the domain expert verifies it against the business rules she knows: refund amounts within tolerance of the original premium, customer notification within the correct window, the reconciliation feed including the corrected transactions. No new failures appear on the corrected path that afternoon. The rolling rate will take several weeks to settle, so the unit keeps the change under observation rather than declaring victory from one clean window. The cycle from noticing the problem to verifying the mechanism in production took less than a day, with no ticket, meeting, or dependency negotiation. The outcome remains under test. That speed, without premature certainty, is the structural consequence of ownership.

Structural correction is organisational design with technological implications, and it belongs at the level of board decision. A board should demand outcomes. Decisions about what the unit builds and how it operates follow from that demand.

What ownership means

A payments unit is a business unit organised around a complete process. The common service-owned model found in large software-dependent corporates organises teams around individual services: one team owns the payments API, another the refund service, another the commission engine, and the payments process remains unowned end to end. The process crosses team boundaries through several software services and data stores, and when something goes wrong it is reconstructed on an incident bridge from memory. No team can change it with confidence, because no team can test or reconcile it as a whole.

An autonomous unit owns the process end to end; its services, data, and contracts exist to implement that process and define how other processes interact with it. The ambiguity that previously required translation disappears. The unit decides what to build, in what order, and with what trade-offs in a single room, where the domain expert's understanding of the business need meets the engineers' understanding of the system with no layer between them. It deploys independently because it interacts with other units through published contracts. It owns its data, which others reach through APIs, events, or data products. It is accountable for continuously measured outcomes such as refund processing time, payment failure rates, reconciliation accuracy, and commission timeliness.

Payments is the easy case: defined inputs, defined outputs, regulatory boundaries that enforce discipline. Other processes have messier boundaries. The first autonomous unit should be chosen to test the model and create the political conditions for extending it to processes where the boundaries are harder to draw.

Why this enables machine-readable reality

Reconciliation requires a single process owner: when no one owns the process, no one maintains the definition, and the reader has nothing to test against. AI can accelerate extraction of hidden rules and code paths from a legacy system, but the result still requires domain correction. Autonomy is the precondition for machine-readable reality.

What makes autonomy real

Each unit operates under a binding charter that joins accountability to authority over five domains: deployment, prioritisation, budget, capability composition, and inter-unit contracts. It also records legal and corporate constraints, escalation thresholds, audit rights, and central bodies that have no ordinary approval role. Chapter 12 specifies the charter and its first ninety days.

The unit's obligations to other units are fixed in explicit, agreed contracts. Inside those contracts it can change its own process and internal design without seeking permission; a change that breaks a contract must be negotiated. It owns the process definition, the software and agents that implement it, its operational data, supplier participation, and the exposure path through which changes reach customers or operations.

It also owns capability composition. The accountable leader decides how the work is performed through employees, agents, deterministic automation, purchased services, outsourcing, or partnerships. That includes organisation design, staffing, skills, leadership, supplier choices, first-line risk and controls, economics, performance, and the leading and lagging indicators by which the result is judged. The unit may outsource work. It cannot outsource ownership of the outcome or its risks.

Authority becomes real when the charter survives its first challenge. Until then, “autonomous” is a claim. A unit that must return to a change board, portfolio committee, or central function for ordinary decisions remains a delivery team inside the old model.

What the unit contains

Each unit is assembled around a process. It combines domain, engineering, data, reliability, and leadership capability inside the same boundary, with one set of process outcomes. The board's concern is whether the capabilities and delegated operating authority required to own the outcome are inside the unit.

The domain specialist and engineers are different experts working on one operating mechanism. Operations may know its exceptions, commercial its promises, finance its economics, risk its constraints, and engineering the behaviour and changeability of the systems through which it runs. Shared ownership preserves one accountable leader and explicit decision rights while requiring every expert to understand the whole outcome directly rather than through a translation role.

False autonomy

The language of “autonomous teams” has been adopted widely, and it often describes cosmetic freedom. A team that can deploy independently while another group controls its data structure still depends on permission. Owning a service leaves the process that service implements fragmented.

A team may deploy several times a day while four services share its data model and every schema change requires an impact assessment. The code change can take forty minutes while the permission takes weeks. A dashboard that celebrates deployment frequency but omits the wait for authority is measuring activity inside false autonomy.

Real autonomy requires that the unit own the process end to end, the logic, the data, the services, the contracts, and the outcomes, and that changes need no permission from other units as long as published contracts are honoured. Trade-offs stay local, and failures become more visible and attributable within the unit. Granting that means accepting that a unit can act without escalation and decide how to implement the CEO's strategic priority. A programme manager negotiating across twelve teams no longer mediates it. That directness is unwelcome in organisations where authority flows from narrative instead of from ownership.

Process ownership comes first

Common service boundaries are drawn around data entities, technical layers, or team convenience. In the autonomous unit model, services follow process boundaries: if the process changes, the services change, and the boundary is maintained through contracts instead of coordination. This model shares structural DNA with established alternatives: Team Topologies' stream-aligned teams, domain-driven design's bounded contexts, and data mesh's distributed data ownership, each reaching process-aligned boundaries and contract-based interaction through a different argument.

What this model adds is an enforcement mechanism: machine-readable artefacts as a governance instrument, AI reconciliation as continuous audit, and a governance charter as political defence against reabsorption. Without process ownership producing binding artefacts a reader can inspect, a well-drawn boundary may remain a design intention that the next reorganisation can erase.

No named organisation supplies an observed history of this complete synthesis. Run one unit under a real charter for two quarters, and use the outcome data to decide whether to extend, revise, or retire the model.

What the board should understand

A board does not need to administer microservices, data models, or deployment pipelines.

Director's shorthand: what an autonomous unit is An autonomous unit is the smallest structure that can be held accountable for a complete business outcome. What it produces: a versioned process definition and published contracts with other units, both tied to continuous outcome metrics. These operating artefacts replace the second reporting narrative. How a director knows it is real: when the board asks “how is payments performing?” the answer comes from a single unit with a single owner, without a negotiated narrative assembled from multiple teams. When a person leaves, the artefacts remain. The test: can the unit deploy an ordinary change inside its process boundary without seeking another team's permission? If yes, ownership may be real. If no, the reorganisation is cosmetic.

Creating autonomous units requires structural change: reassigning ownership, collapsing coordination roles, granting real authority. The affected functions have rational incentives to restore the controls and responsibilities they previously held. Sustained board authorisation is what prevents that restoration from becoming governance capture.
