End “The Business”

The price of vagueness has changed

People close to how a large organisation actually works experience a particular kind of exhaustion once that organisation runs on software. It is quiet and corrosive, the feeling of navigating representations everyone treats as real while knowing that they are not.

You sit in meetings where “the business” discusses priorities that have no stable relationship to the systems that produce value. You watch strategy documents grow longer as understanding shrinks. You see engineers blamed for outcomes that were decided elsewhere, in abstractions disconnected from reality. You notice that the more precisely you describe reality, the more friction you generate.

A specific organisational form that has become widespread over the last two decades creates this exhaustion: the software-dependent corporate.

Software-dependent corporates are large, established organisations whose products and operations now depend critically on software. Their power structures, budgeting models, and decision-making processes were designed for a world in which software was a support function: something procured from vendors, managed through an IT department, and governed as a cost centre. Banks, insurers, retailers, logistics firms, airlines, public sector bodies, and older companies that describe themselves as “technology companies” but still operate with these inherited assumptions all fall into this category.

Software-dependent corporates have run software for decades. The structural lag widened when smartphones made software the default customer interface, cloud computing made custom development at scale economically viable, and continuous software operation became an ordinary requirement. These organisations hired engineers by the hundreds and adopted modern delivery practices. Their governance structures, budget cycles, HR frameworks, and executive mental models remained behind. Technology changed, but the power structures did not, and the gap has been compounding ever since.

Most will readily acknowledge that they have legacy software. They commission reports on technical debt, fund migration programmes, and talk openly about the need to modernise their technology estate. “Legacy” is an accepted category for systems. It implies something that was once fit for purpose but has been outgrown. Suggest, however, that the governance structures are equally legacy, that the budgeting model, the HR framework, and the separation of “the business” from engineering are design choices inherited from a previous era, and the conversation stops. The reason is that “legacy” implies something that needs to be replaced, and the people who benefit from the current structures are the people who would need to authorise their replacement.

Branding does not matter here. What matters is whether software is the medium through which the organisation creates and delivers value, and whether the governance structures have caught up with that reality. In software-dependent corporates, the answer to the first question is yes and the answer to the second is no. Software mediates everything that matters: how customers are served, how products are built, how operations run. Yet governance still treats it as a support function even though it is the system through which the business exists.

Language provides the most reliable diagnostic. If your organisation talks about “the business” as a group of people who are not engineers, this book is about your company. If engineers are considered to work in the business by default, it probably is not.

Do not repair the phrase by calling everyone else a business stakeholder. Name the expertise. An operations specialist understands the operating exceptions. A marketer understands demand and positioning. A commercial lead understands the customer and the deal. Finance, risk, security, data, design, and engineering each bring a different view of the same process and outcome. Engineering, data, AI, and cybersecurity are business capabilities because the company cannot operate, compete, or exercise control without them. The useful boundary is between different kinds of expertise, not between technology and an imaginary remainder called the business.

Once “the business” is defined to exclude its engineers, maintaining one account of reality becomes optional, and the people closest to it suffer first.

Throughout this book, people closest to reality refers to those whose effectiveness depends on clear ownership, explicit constraints, and direct contact with real systems and customer outcomes. They are often engineers, but they also exist in product, design, data, and leadership roles. Their effectiveness deteriorates when ambiguity is rewarded and truth is negotiated. The claims handler knows every process exception the system does not encode. The operations analyst uses a shadow spreadsheet to reconcile what the official reports cannot. The compliance officer carries the real regulatory interpretation in her head because the documented version has not been updated in three years. These people are closest to reality because their work collapses without accurate information, whatever their job titles.

Reality-bound people do not necessarily perform well in software-dependent corporates; the system often prevents it, and their frustration is a structural consequence of an environment where representations of work are treated as substitutes for reality. The illusions that follow, the narratives that replace reality, the fake products, the aspirational architectures, the processes never written down, are intelligent coping mechanisms that serve specific purposes.

Cheap AI has changed the economics of clarity, and the cost trajectory is unlikely to reverse, though the shift is far from complete: most organisations are currently absorbing AI into the machinery they already run. Reality becomes harder to negotiate away as reconciliation gets cheaper. The people who could make AI work, those with the domain knowledge, the precision, the understanding of how the system behaves, are the same people these structures have been exhausting since the early 2010s. Many were driven out precisely as the organisation began to need them most.

Autonomous agents now deployed inside these systems need exactly what reality-bound people were asking for: processes defined precisely enough to execute, boundaries clear enough to respect, ownership explicit enough to navigate. Humans compensated through social negotiation. Agents cannot reliably reconstruct missing authority that way, so an organisation that cannot describe itself clearly enough for its own people cannot describe itself clearly enough for an agent either.

If you have been punished for precision, exhausted by coordination, or silenced because your understanding of the system was unwelcome, you will recognise the diagnosis that follows: why software-dependent corporates reliably produce illusions of work, and how those illusions turn into activity without progress. The correction comes after it: a structure in which those experiences are no longer required to keep ordinary work moving. None of it is a call for better communication, more alignment, or another round of transformation.

What the correction is. It begins with one business process and one unit that owns it end to end. The unit keeps a versioned description of the process, publishes its dependencies as contracts, and reconciles the description against the running system. The purpose is to create evidence that survives attempts to absorb it as one person's opinion. The rest of this book develops the unit, the technical model that lets it ship, the charter that protects it, and the transition it makes possible. The contribution is the combination: persistent business-process ownership; binding, machine-readable artefacts; continuous reconciliation against operational behaviour; and explicit political protection against organisational reabsorption. Each component has a lineage. Their operation as one governance system is the hypothesis this book proposes.

What exists now. No single product performs the complete model described in this book. Current tools can analyse code and documents, validate schemas, and compare a defined process with a bounded implementation under human review. Continuous detection of divergence across artefacts still requires engineering, while causal tracing across units is not yet dependable. The artefacts are the durable layer; models and vendors remain replaceable.

The argument is cumulative: the diagnosis establishes why the correction is necessary, AI changes the economics, and the later chapters develop the structure, machinery, governance, and recovery path. The destination is not a company without constraint or coordination. It is a company in which coordination is the exception produced by a real boundary, constraint is explicit, and the people with the best knowledge of the mechanism can use it to improve the outcome.

The prologue's suppressed reconciliation is what this structural lag looks like after the cost of synthesis collapses. The question is no longer whether the contradictions can be assembled. It is whether the company will give assembled reality authority.
