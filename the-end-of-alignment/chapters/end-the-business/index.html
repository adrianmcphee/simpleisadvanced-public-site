End “The Business”

The price of vagueness has changed

People close to how a large organisation actually works experience a particular kind of exhaustion once that organisation runs on software. It is quiet and corrosive, the feeling of navigating representations everyone treats as real while knowing that they are not.

You sit in meetings where “the business” discusses priorities that have no stable relationship to the systems that produce value. You watch strategy documents grow longer as understanding shrinks. You see engineers blamed for outcomes that were decided elsewhere, in abstractions disconnected from reality. You notice that the more precisely you describe reality, the more friction you generate.

A specific organisational form that has become widespread over the last two decades creates this exhaustion: the software-dependent corporate.

Software-dependent corporates are large, established organisations whose products and operations now depend critically on software. Their power structures, budgets, and decisions were designed when software was a support function. It was procured from vendors, managed through an IT department, and governed as a cost centre. Banks, insurers, retailers, logistics firms, airlines, public sector bodies, and older companies that describe themselves as “technology companies” but still operate with these inherited assumptions all fall into this category.

Most will acknowledge legacy software. Suggest that the budgeting model, governance structure, and separation of “the business” from engineering are equally legacy, and the conversation becomes less enthusiastic. Technology changed; the distribution of authority largely did not.

Branding does not matter here. What matters is whether software is the medium through which the organisation creates and delivers value, and whether the governance structures have caught up with that reality. In software-dependent corporates, the answer to the first question is yes and the answer to the second is no. Software mediates everything that matters: how customers are served, how products are built, how operations run. Yet governance still treats it as a support function even though it is the system through which the business exists.

Language provides the most reliable diagnostic. If your organisation talks about “the business” as a group of people who are not engineers, this book is about your company. If engineers are considered to work in the business by default, it probably is not.

If engineers do not work in “the business”, where do they work? In Technology, apparently: somewhere outside the business that consumes a material share of its revenue while building and operating the systems through which that revenue arrives. The company then spends more on product managers, OKRs, quarterly planning and operating-model consultants to keep the two sides aligned.

Do not repair the phrase by calling everyone else a business stakeholder. Name the expertise. An operations specialist understands the operating exceptions. A marketer understands demand and positioning. A commercial lead understands the customer and the deal. Finance, risk, security, data, design, and engineering each bring a different view of the same process and outcome. Engineering, data, AI, and cybersecurity are business capabilities because the company cannot operate, compete, or exercise control without them. The useful boundary is between different kinds of expertise, not between technology and an imaginary remainder called the business.

Author observation

At one company I helped, engineers referred to Product as “the business”. Operations and Sales were “the business business”.

The terminology had discovered the operating model more accurately than the organisation chart. Product translated between Engineering and the functions selling and operating the service. Engineering received requirements from Product; Product reconstructed them from conversations with Operations and Sales; Operations and Sales discovered what the system meant when a change returned. The underlying process ran through all four functions, but they did not maintain one shared description or a stable language for its important states and decisions.

Product was not the cause. Capable people had been placed in the middle to compensate for a boundary the organisation preserved. The company had not integrated Engineering into the business. It had pluralised the business.

Once “the business” excludes its engineers, maintaining one account of reality becomes optional. The people who cannot work without accurate information suffer first. They include the claims handler who knows the exceptions the system omits, the operations analyst reconciling official reports in a shadow spreadsheet, and the engineer whose change fails when an undocumented rule appears. Their job titles differ. Their work is bound to the running company rather than its presentation.

AI makes their precision more valuable. Agents need the same things they were asking for: processes defined well enough to execute, boundaries clear enough to respect, and ownership explicit enough to navigate. Humans compensated for their absence through social negotiation. Agents reproduce the missing decisions at machine speed.

If you have been punished for precision, exhausted by coordination, or silenced because your understanding of the system was unwelcome, you will recognise the diagnosis that follows. Software-dependent corporates reliably produce illusions of work, which become activity without progress. The correction is a structure in which those experiences are no longer required to keep ordinary work moving. None of it is a call for better communication, more alignment, or another round of transformation.

The correction begins with one business process and one unit that owns it end to end. The unit keeps a versioned description of the process, publishes its dependencies as contracts, and reconciles that description against the running system. The destination is not a company without constraint or coordination. It is one in which coordination marks a real boundary, constraints are explicit, and the people who understand the mechanism can improve its outcome.
