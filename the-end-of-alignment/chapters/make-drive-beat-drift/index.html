Make Drive Beat Drift

Most roadmaps are lists of intended activity arranged against time. They look like plans because the columns are quarters. They become contracts because somebody approved them. Then reality arrives, the premises change, and the organisation faces a choice it is structurally bad at making: keep delivering the approved list after it has stopped being the best route, or quietly change the list while continuing to report progress against it.

The first choice produces disciplined waste. The second produces unmanaged drift. Companies alternate between them and call the resulting oscillation agile planning.

An agentic operating model needs a different control object. Agents make it cheap to decompose work, explore alternatives, generate code, and maintain several plausible paths at once. A long implementation backlog becomes less useful as this capacity grows. A backlog full of agent-ready tasks can convert ambiguity into output at extraordinary speed. The missing question is whether the output moved the company towards the result it intended to create.

Drive versus Drift is the steering loop for that question.

Build the Strategic Context Stack

The method begins above the roadmap. Strategic clarity needs a load-bearing structure, not a slogan. Purpose, vision, mission, goals, strategy, bets, OKRs, and KPIs do different jobs at different horizons. Collapsing them into a wall of management language removes the distinctions needed to steer and creates the ambiguity that alignment meetings are then hired to repair.

The Strategic Context Stack is one practical design. Its horizons are defaults rather than a corporate calendar; a regulated infrastructure company and a young consumer product will revise at different rates. The essential discipline is that higher layers remain stable enough to constrain the faster layers beneath them, while every layer has a named authority and a declared reason to change.

At a ten-to-twenty-year horizon, a vision gives the organisation a direction that survives annual planning. It is useful only if it rules something out. A sentence compatible with every possible acquisition, product, market, and operating model is advertising. The mission turns that direction into a five-year destination. Goals make the destination falsifiable over the next one to three years. A vision without a mission permits drift; a mission without measurable goals permits reinterpretation.

Strategy then names the mechanism expected to achieve those goals and the causal logic behind it. “Grow through partnerships” is a decision. “Grow through partners because trusted distribution is the binding constraint, and because the expected volume makes the unit economics viable” is a strategy whose assumptions can be tested. Strategy implies choices and exclusions. It changes when evidence falsifies its mechanism, not when the commitment becomes uncomfortable or a new executive wants different slides.

Bets fund bounded tests of that strategy. OKRs instrument the leading signals the causal logic predicts. KPIs monitor the health of the running process. A list of priorities is not a strategy because it contains no causal claim. An OKR is not a strategy because it measures a bounded bet. A KPI is not an OKR because operational health and strategic learning are different questions. If the same number answers both, a failed hypothesis can be disguised as poor execution and an operational failure can be excused as experimentation.

Before a unit adopts a target, it must know the present distribution, the provenance and reliability of the data, the observation window, the process expected to move it, and the strategic mechanism being tested. The people who can change that process, including its engineers, must inspect the same evidence. Cascading OKRs without those conditions reproduces the business-and-technology split in matching vocabulary: one group owns the narrative, another receives a delivery target, and both report alignment against a baseline nobody established.

Each layer should therefore be an independently readable, versioned artefact with a named owner, a revision rule, and explicit references to the layers above and below it. A current bet identifies the strategy it tests and the goal it serves. A unit milestone identifies either the bet it advances or the explicit operating obligation it satisfies, together with the customer promise and process involved. Production evidence traces back through that chain. Ambiguity goes to the authority that owns the affected layer; it does not summon everyone to reconstruct the hierarchy socially.

A connected, readable stack ends alignment as a recurring activity. Alignment becomes a read operation. People can discover the current direction and the logic behind it without attending the offsite or knowing what was agreed at dinner. Autonomous units receive constraints that are strong enough to coordinate action without a centre prescribing their task lists. Genuine choices still require argument and judgement, but meetings handle new decisions rather than repeatedly recovering old ones.

An AI reader makes the test unforgiving. It can ask whether active bets test the stated strategy, whether unit milestones serve those bets or a named operating obligation, whether OKRs measure the predicted leading indicators, and whether KPIs remained healthy while the strategy advanced. It can also expose a vision, mission, strategy deck, OKR set, and roadmap that describe different companies. If neither a new employee nor a machine can trace the current milestone to corporate direction or a binding operational duty, the roadmap is an inventory of wishes and alignment remains a tax on ambiguity.

The milestone is stable; the proof moves

The current milestone is the central artefact. The backlog serves it.

A milestone defines a complete, observable outcome. It names the actor, the initiating gesture, the resulting change in the world, the process and system boundaries involved, the exclusions, and the evidence required to call the result real. It includes enough product and process context to reject an attractive local improvement that weakens the whole.

A milestone stated as “launch automated refunds” sounds directional and is almost useless. Does automated mean no human touch for the normal path, or merely a generated payment file? Which customers qualify? What happens when the payment provider is unavailable? Who owns reconciliation? What is the maximum delay? Does the customer receive a confirmed result or only a status page? What proves that money arrived rather than an API returning success?

A serious milestone answers those questions at the level needed to keep the outcome coherent. It does not pretend to know every implementation step. That distinction matters. Execution without a clear milestone is efficient wandering. Execution against a milestone rewritten whenever the work becomes difficult is disguised replanning.

The milestone should remain stable while the proof changes frequently. Proof Synthesis is the current reconciliation of milestone intent, present process and product truth, and exact evidence. It asks what is proven, what is partially evidenced, what remains open, what is blocked, and which next move produces the highest gain.

AI earns a central role in Proof Synthesis. It can read a changing body of artefacts and keep the differences visible. It can find that the UI path exists but the recovery path does not, that the provider contract covers retries but not duplicate payments, or that the deployed candidate differs from the version the test report describes. It can propose the next three to five moves. It cannot decide that the customer promise should be weakened or that an unowned risk is acceptable. The relevant product and process authorities still own meaning.

Drive is what became true

Drive is evidenced net movement towards the milestone. It asks what became newly true and ignores volume without consequence.

A customer can complete a journey that previously broke. A critical domain ambiguity has an owner ruling and an implemented meaning. A risky seam now works end to end and fails safely. A supplier path has tested continuity and recovery. An old contract version has no remaining consumers and can be removed. Evidence has falsified an expensive approach early enough to stop funding it.

Those are movements. Commits, tickets, agent invocations, generated lines, meetings, utilisation, and story points are activity. Activity may be necessary, but it does not establish Drive.

This sounds obvious until an organisation introduces agents. An engineering group can suddenly produce five times as many changes, demonstrations, documents, and experimental branches. The visible energy is intoxicating. Without a stable outcome and exact proof, local output overwhelms the ability to judge the system. The company becomes faster at producing unresolved alternatives. It experiences acceleration while the product experiences noise.

Drift is the bill attached to movement

Drift is deviation introduced or allowed to persist relative to the milestone and its governing context. Outcome drift weakens the promised result. Experience drift breaks the joined customer flow. Terminology drift gives the same state several meanings. Process drift separates operation from the accepted process. Architecture and contract drift weaken ownership or compatibility. Verification drift presents local confidence as end-to-end proof. Production drift separates the served system from the verified one. Authority drift lets a person, provider, or agent act outside declared rights.

Some Drift is deliberate. A unit may accept a temporary compatibility layer to migrate consumers, a manual control while a provider path is repaired, or a feature flag that keeps unfinished behaviour dark. Deliberate Drift has an owner, consequence, expiry, and retirement condition. Unnamed Drift is lost control.

Drive versus Drift should not become a synthetic score. The moment headquarters ranks units by a number called “net drive,” the number will become a performance theatre. The judgement is qualitative but disciplined:

What became newly true? What material Drift was introduced, removed, or consciously accepted? What evidence changed confidence in the milestone? Was net directional gain positive, negative, or indeterminate? What is the next highest-yield move? Which decision, if any, belongs to a product, process, contract, risk, or portfolio authority?

A high-output week can be net negative when it creates more semantic and operational debt than the movement is worth. A quiet week can have exceptional Drive when it resolves the contradiction that blocked the whole path or proves that an attractive route should be abandoned.

Work backwards, one complete slice at a time

Proof Synthesis may produce several candidate moves. They provide sequencing options while the unit chooses the next independently useful vertical slice: the smallest change that moves the complete outcome, removes material uncertainty, preserves boundaries, can be integrated safely, and produces proof.

The default is one ordinary implementation slice per autonomous unit. Agents do not repeal work-in-progress limits. They make those limits more important because the cost of beginning work has collapsed while the cost of integrating meaning has not.

At the end of the slice, the unit reruns the joined proof, updates process and product truth, records any remaining Drift, and only then selects the next move. Outcomes and evidence order the roadmap, allowing it to change when the system teaches the team something.

The approach remains a form of planning, but it is honest about where planning has value. More effort goes into the destination, boundaries, failure behaviour, and proof. Less goes into pretending the entire route can be enumerated before the first contact with reality.

A portfolio steers without taking the wheel

A portfolio contains product theses, customer promises, process capabilities, and strategic bets. Each product has a vision of the customer future it intends to create. One product may depend on several process-owning units; one unit may fulfil promises for several products. Those views must be traceable without being flattened into one enormous roadmap.

Each autonomous unit owns its outcome roadmap inside the company's strategic commitments. The roadmap orders milestones by outcome instead of dating an inventory of features. Product leadership preserves the promise and cross-process experience. Portfolio leadership decides which bets receive capital, which products should be created, combined, or retired, what cross-unit outcomes matter, and which broad sequence best serves the whole. It inspects milestone movement, strategic learning, cross-unit seams, cost, and material Drift. Ticket volumes and one central backlog cannot substitute for those decisions.

A cross-unit customer outcome creates local milestones in the participating units and one temporary compatibility sequence across their contracts. The temporary coordinating role retires when the joined journey works and the contracts are stable. If it persists as a programme office, the company has converted one customer outcome into another permanent translation layer.

When a milestone genuinely needs revision, the relevant authority changes it explicitly. The previous target and its evidence remain in history. New information can justify a different promise or a lower expected return. Difficulty alone does not. A milestone weakened so that current work can be called complete is scope erosion wearing the language of learning.

Drive versus Drift makes strategy operational without turning it into a plan for every action. It gives agents a stable destination, gives units freedom over the route, gives leaders evidence of movement, and gives governance a record of deviations and decisions. Velocity no longer gets to serve as its own argument.

The question at the end of a week is no longer, “How much did we deliver?” It is, “Did the running company move towards the one we intend to build, and what did that movement cost us in Drift?”
