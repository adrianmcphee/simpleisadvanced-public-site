Tell the Truth About Who Loses

The dysfunction has measurable costs. It also sustains roles, authority and commercial demand that its correction would diminish.

Between diagnosis and correction, there is resistance. It is predictable, rational, and a recurrent reason structural corrections fail. Naming it is a precondition for surviving it.

Who loses

The roles that lose scope were rational responses to a real problem. An organisation rewarded the visible coordination of complexity while neglecting its elimination, because complexity itself was invisible to the people who controlled headcount. The structure can change while the roles remain in place.

Programme directors and portfolio leads. Their authority derives from coordinating across teams that do not share ownership. When units own processes and interact through contracts, that coordination disappears. That work was real; its purpose has disappeared.

Central data teams. Their authority derives from controlling a shared substrate. When units own their data and publish through contracts, the central team's scope shrinks to reporting-layer governance: contract compatibility, cross-unit standards, conflict resolution. For a team that built its identity around controlling the organisation's data, a smaller, more focused function is experienced as a loss.

Product managers of portfolio labels. Their role bridges a portfolio label and the system. When the label is separated into an honest product promise and owned processes, the bridge is unnecessary. People with customer knowledge, domain depth, commercial judgement, or credible product authority can move into the unit or own the promise directly. People whose role consists principally of backlog administration and stakeholder mediation face a harder truth: the coordination capability may be real, but the corrected structure should no longer need it as a permanent job.

Coordination-dependent executives. Some executive roles derive authority from controlling information flow without owning outcomes. A role built around synthesising across functions and managing the upward narrative loses interpretive insulation when artefacts become machine-readable and outcomes become traceable. Repairing the boundary removes more than meetings; it removes the stage on which the role performs leadership. A week spent convening conflicts the role cannot resolve places the role inside the structural problem. This is the most politically sensitive category, because the people occupying these roles may have the authority to block the change.

The Alignment-Industrial Complex. The consulting ecosystem earns revenue from the coordination layer persisting. The buyer reinforces the incentive: a CEO who commissions a strategic alignment review needs an output that is presentable and safe. “Improve cross-functional alignment through a governance framework” fits; “grant one unit complete ownership of the claims process and remove the programme management office's authority over it” does not. The external and internal scripts are identical because the incentive is identical.

A programme director at a European insurer watches the first autonomous unit's quarterly results: claims resolution time down 31%, cost per claim down €14, forty-three deployments in the quarter. His own programme spans six teams, none of which owns a complete process, and his last status report was assembled by a delivery lead who left last month and hasn't been replaced.

He isn't hostile to the model. He can see it works, and he can see that his role exists because the organisation hasn't done what the unit demonstrates is possible. His programme produces status reports; the unit produces outcomes. He begins drafting a proposal for a governance review of the unit's deployment cadence. The proposal isn't cynical: for fifteen years the organisation rewarded coordination as a form of value, and the instinct to coordinate is a professional reflex, not a conscious choice. What he hasn't named, even to himself, is that the competence the organisation now requires isn't the one he has spent fifteen years building.

The programme director is genuinely good at his job. For fifteen years he held the claims process together across six teams. He knows where the exceptions live, which handoffs fail silently, which integration points break under load on the last Friday of every month. His role disappears once the structure finally works. In month one he maps exception paths the documentation never captured and identifies three failure modes the unit would have hit the hard way. By month two the contract model handles the questions he used to answer, and his calendar empties. In month three HR offers him a “change management lead” title with no charter, no boundary, and no measurable outcome, a holding pattern that defers a conversation no one wants to have.

His sponsor had already offered something better in week six. The choices were a role inside the second unit where his knowledge of exception paths has direct operational value, a redeployment where his skills match a genuine need, or a separation that acknowledges the claims process survived because of what he did. He chooses the second unit. That choice shows the difference between honesty and avoidance dressed in HR vocabulary.

If you are in the middle

Most readers affected by this transition will be neither the sponsor nor the unit lead. They will carry responsibility for a slice of work while lacking the authority to redraw its boundary. Waiting for the reorganisation leaves them exposed; pretending to possess authority they have not been granted creates a shadow operating model that collapses at the first contested decision.

The useful move is to make one part of the mechanism legible. Trace a recent customer or operator outcome through the process, systems, handoffs, decisions, and evidence that produced it. Record where ownership changes, where approval waits, and which recurring meeting compensates for an undefined boundary. Preserve the source of each claim. A sponsor can act on that artefact; granting autonomy still requires an explicit decision.

Then ask for a bounded decision. Name the process slice, the outcome, the authority required, the constraints that remain, the adjacent contract, and the period of the test. Ask the accountable executive to grant or refuse that authority explicitly. A refusal is information: it identifies the boundary or veto that the represented organisation usually hides. Keep the waiting time and added coordination visible rather than absorbing them through personal effort.

Protect the human position with the same honesty. Ask what role, decision right, and measurable outcome will exist after the transition. A holding-pattern title without a charter delays the answer. Domain knowledge about exceptions, customers, suppliers, regulation, or failure modes is valuable when attached to an owned process. Coordination skill may transfer into unit leadership, contract design, incident command, or portfolio judgement when those are genuine needs. The person deserves a specific destination or a fair separation; reassurance without either is another narrative standing in for an operating fact.

What resistance sounds like

Resistance rarely presents as opposition; it presents as prudence, in phrases that recur across industries with the precision of a shared script. “We need more alignment before we proceed” delays action until the coordination layer reasserts itself. “Let's pilot it in a contained environment” restricts scope until improvement is invisible, then cites the absence as evidence. “The organisation isn't ready for this level of change” treats readiness as a condition to establish first, although readiness emerges through the work. “We should get buy-in from all stakeholders first” gives every affected party a veto, including those whose roles the change would eliminate. “Can we see the ROI case before restructuring?” demands the new model prove itself within the old model's measurement framework, which cannot see the costs the new model addresses. Each phrase is individually reasonable; together they reconstitute the coordination layer the change was designed to eliminate.

How resistance derails

Death by pilot. The unit is approved with deliberately constrained scope. After two quarters, the review concludes it “shows promise but doesn't justify broader adoption”: the pilot delivered exactly the evidence for the conclusion written before it started.

Governance capture. Governance forums add individually justified deployment and data reviews alongside alignment meetings, until the unit spends more time reporting than operating. Governance capture is a common failure mode because each erosion is hard to see in isolation, and its end state is false autonomy.

Reframe as technology project. The structural change is reclassified as a technology initiative subject to the annual budget cycle. When the budget is cut in year two, the change dies.

Sponsor removal. The sponsor leaves the role or acquires competing priorities. The replacement arrives without context, and within two quarters the unit operates under the old model with new terminology.

How to neutralise

Resistance can be made expensive.

Charter enforcement with built-in defence. The governance charter must contain its own enforcement mechanism: a named escalation path, a dispute resolution clause with a fixed five-day window, and a protection clause preventing governance bodies from adding obligations without the sponsor's written approval.

Named escalation paths with fixed resolution windows. The proposed charter allows five business days to resolve a dispute, after which the unit proceeds unless a named legal, safety, or risk threshold requires a hold. Without the clock and explicit exceptions, ordinary requests expand to fill the governance calendar.

Board-level sustained authority. Sponsors change roles. Board-level endorsement, renewed quarterly, gives the charter an authority that does not disappear with one sponsor.

Making resistance costs visible. When a forum adds an obligation, the cost is documented: person-hours consumed and deployment delay imposed, attributed to the forum, and reported to the sponsor. Visibility gives resistance a price tag.

The programme director submits his governance review proposal, which is declined on the basis of the charter. He will spend the next six months discovering whether he can build value inside a structure that no longer needs coordination as a form of value. His answer is not yet written.

The political cost of correction is real. Leaving the structure untouched also has a cost, distributed and invisible until tested externally.

Holding the line after the first unit

Each executive role is affected differently by structural correction: what stops being governable by narrative, what new artefacts become decisive, what to stop rewarding, and what to authorise. First, the shift that applies to all of them.

The structural shift

Senior executive work often operates at a distance from mechanism: synthesising across functions, weighing trade-offs, and allocating capital without holding every operational detail. Distance is how leadership scales, and over time it can become identity. When processes become explicit and contracts become binding, machine-readable artefacts shorten the path between commitment and consequence, and executive distance stops working the same way. One executive experiences this as increased control; another experiences a loss of interpretive insulation, the capacity to reframe outcomes, diffuse responsibility, and manage contradiction through narrative while avoiding mechanism. In some systems, that capacity becomes part of what the organisation rewards and promotes. Boards can participate in the resistance by preferring a smoothed narrative to confronting structural problems they have tolerated.

What the first year looks like

Quarter & Structural action & Evidence the board receives & Failure signal \ Q1 & Map three to five critical processes and their system dependencies & Named owners; process-and-system overlay; concentration of shared state & Capability maps are substituted for actual processes \ Q2 & Launch the first unit under a binding charter & Baseline metrics; first reconciliation; record of the charter's first challenge & The sponsor equivocates when authority is tested \ Q3–Q4 & Form adjacent units through explicit contract boundaries & Reduced coordination; contract-resolution time; process outcomes & Central obligations begin accumulating and unit time returns to reporting \

The first year succeeds when authority has moved, boundaries have held, and outcomes have changed. A new vocabulary with the same approval paths amounts to relabelling.

Structural correction has a human cost, and the organisation owes displaced people honesty: redeployment into a unit where their process knowledge has value, or an honest separation. Six months of role ambiguity merely delays the same outcome.

False autonomy after early success

A sponsor can leave after early improvements, and the replacement can treat the charter as an anomaly. Deployment authority then routes back through a change board, prioritisation returns to the quarterly planning cycle, and capability is reassigned. The charter remains on paper after its authority has gone. Calling the result evidence that autonomy “doesn't work at our scale” confuses a reabsorbed unit with the model it no longer implements.

A subtler pattern is false autonomy: binding authority eroded into advisory authority through the accumulation of individually reasonable governance requests, each justified as risk management, none challenged. It leaves a pattern in the board's own instruments. One useful test is quantitative. A board should ask the unit lead what percentage of the team's time is spent on obligations imposed by central functions. A sustained increase after the charter was signed is a warning that advisory obligations are becoming approval rights and the coordination layer is reconstituting itself behind the vocabulary of ownership. The pattern deserves investigation before the dysfunction becomes harder to diagnose and easier to narrate as progress.

What changes for the executive team

Structural correction changes what each executive role operates on; the shift is from narrative to artefact, from interpretive latitude to falsifiable commitment. For the CEO, strategic claims become testable against continuous measurement. For the CFO, envelope budgets connect spending to outcomes. Business leaders take capability and organisation design back from a central People strategy; Employment Operations provides the service and institutional governance enforces lawful authority. For the CTO, architecture and organisational design become the same discipline. For the board, management packs require reconciliation evidence before they reach the boardroom. Authority previously exercised through interpretation must now be exercised through evidence.

The shift for a CFO is concrete. A unit may allocate more of its envelope to reliability than the product roadmap implied. The old model asks the CFO to approve that input. The new model asks whether resolution time, cost, risk, and customer outcomes support the choice after it becomes observable. Authority moves from approving each means to judging whether delegated capital produced the promised result.

For the executive currently responsible for the People function, the strategic mandate does not survive intact. Capability and organisation design move to the leaders who own the processes. Employment Operations remains a service, and employee relations moves into institutional governance. During the transition, those functions help identify which coordination roles carry transferable domain knowledge and which exist only because structural incoherence required them. The owning executives then choose honest redeployment into a unit or honest separation. A board should ask whether every role the transition removes has a concrete disposition, or merely a holding-pattern title. The answer shows whether leadership is managing the human consequence or deferring it behind People vocabulary.

Year two: what sustaining looks like

Elena, the audit committee chair, reads the quarterly reconciliation report at month fourteen. The renewals unit's Drift flags have risen while the claims unit's have fallen for the third consecutive quarter. The increase is the report working as designed; the renewals unit is surfacing the gaps that typically appear while its artefacts and system are first reconciled. She asks one question in the board meeting: why is the renewals unit's remediation rate lower than the claims unit's was at the same stage? The CTO gives a specific, testable answer: the renewals unit shares a contract boundary with an external provider whose response times are outside its control.

The sponsor who launched the first unit has moved to a different division, and the second wave of resistance has arrived, quieter than the first: a gradual recalibration of expectations downward. Elena does not need to adjudicate the technical merits. She has the reconciliation report, and the pattern is legible because the artefacts exist. She authorises the continuation after reading the evidence herself.

The report measures institutional outcomes; it does not capture what changed for the people the structure displaced. The programme director who chose the renewals unit over the holding-pattern title is six months into the role, and his knowledge of exception paths and failure modes is now embedded in the unit's process definition. His contribution is measurable and attributable.

Each executive role faces the same fundamental shift: from governing through narrative to governing through artefact, operating on testable, versioned descriptions and leaving negotiated interpretations behind. Clarity has exposed the cost of comfort, and whether to act on that visibility is now an informed choice. The discomfort comes from recognising the old model as theirs.
