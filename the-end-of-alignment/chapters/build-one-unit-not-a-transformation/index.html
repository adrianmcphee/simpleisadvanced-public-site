Build One Unit, Not a Transformation

The first autonomous unit holds real authority over its process and cannot be a sandboxed experiment the organisation quietly walks back. It operates under its own governance charter, and its outcomes provide the evaluation. Its job is to produce evidence that cannot be outlasted the way a memo can: one unit operating with genuine autonomy creates selection pressure, making the cost of the surrounding dysfunction visible by contrast.

Selecting the process

Choosing a process for the first autonomous unit is consequential. Spending six months selecting the perfect candidate recreates the coordination problem. The criteria interact, and no process satisfies them all. Choose one that can test the design while making the remaining risk explicit.

A process must have clear boundaries. A self-contained process, one whose inputs, outputs, and decision points can be defined without requiring the whole organisation to redraw its lines, is viable. A process deeply entangled with every other will consume the unit's first six months in boundary negotiations and leave no time to operate. Clear boundaries make dependencies identifiable, countable, and small enough for published contracts to govern.

A process must also have measurable outcomes tied to its behaviour: cost per transaction, time to resolution, error rates, customer satisfaction, and revenue attributable to the journey. Tickets closed or features shipped reveal only activity. Without measurable outcomes the unit cannot demonstrate its value, and without demonstrated value the organisational immune system reclaims the territory within two quarters.

The process must also matter to the current strategic context. The first unit should be able to name the goal, strategic mechanism, and funded bet its outcome advances. Otherwise the experiment can succeed locally while proving nothing the corporation needs to learn. This trace does not give a portfolio committee control of the unit's backlog. It gives the unit a durable reason for existing and gives the sponsor a way to judge whether its autonomy moved the company rather than merely improving an isolated metric.

The process should also be causing visible pain. One that already works well and attracts no complaints makes a poor first unit because the contrast will be invisible. Choose one where the dysfunction is felt: incidents are frequent, resolution takes weeks, customers complain, and engineers are frustrated, because that felt pain is what creates the motivation to change.

Each unit needs a willing and capable lead who already holds the contradictions, understands both the process and the systems, and has been frustrated by the gap between what the organisation says and what it does. Prior experience running an autonomous unit is optional. The lead must understand that autonomy means the unit decides what to work on inside its charter, owns its outcomes, and cannot defer accountability to another team.

A process should have a manageable dependency surface: a small number of integration points with other systems and units, each definable as a contract. If it requires real-time coordination with twelve other groups to function, it is the wrong starting point. Two or three seams that contracts can govern are a useful starting preference whose practicality the first unit can test.

Each unit needs leadership air cover: a named executive sponsor committed to protecting its autonomy through the declared test period and prepared to withstand the first sign of friction. The sponsor expects adjacent authorities to reclaim control and intervenes when they do. The role is to prevent the rest of the organisation from managing the unit back into the old model.

Claims, order fulfilment, payments, billing, and dispatch can all be plausible candidates. The industry label proves nothing. The actual process still needs real value, legible boundaries, measurable pain, and a dependency surface the first unit can govern.

The first three weeks

The unit lead sits down with the domain expert and two engineers to write the process definition for claims management. It is the first working session, and the whiteboard is clean.

Within thirty minutes, they find the first discrepancy. The domain expert describes a triage step: incoming claims are assessed for complexity and routed to the appropriate handler based on claim type, value, and regulatory category. The senior engineer pulls up the service, and the triage logic turns out to be a hard-coded routing table, last updated nineteen months ago, before a regulatory change that added two new claim categories. Those categories were applied in the code as exception handlers that bypass the routing table entirely. The domain expert had never seen the exception handlers, and the engineer had never seen the triage logic the domain expert described.

Both of them are right. The documented process and the implemented process diverged after the regulatory change: the code was patched, the operations manual wasn't, and for nineteen months the claims team has been triaging the two new categories by hand, using a workaround that nobody outside the team knows about.

The domain expert says: “This has been wrong for nineteen months. We have been triaging manually.”

The engineer says: “I didn't know there was a manual process.”

Together they write the first line of the process definition, and it describes what actually happens.

The first line took thirty minutes; the full definition, including every state, transition, failure mode, and contract dependency, took three weeks.

The first three weeks are dangerous because the governance charter is untested and nobody has demonstrated that the model works. The lead must force ambiguity to become explicit, which the old model systematically prevented.

Writing the process definition makes this real. It takes longer than expected, because the domain expert and the engineers will discover, typically in the second or third working session, that they have been operating with different mental models of the same process. The triage logic the domain expert understands from operations and the logic the engineers implemented three years ago will not match. The mismatch is the exercise itself: each discrepancy is a location where the organisation has been absorbing cost invisibly.

Unit leads should expect the first complete draft to take two to four weeks, and should resist the impulse to accelerate it. A definition written quickly describes aspiration. A definition tested by walking through real recent cases, finding the ones it does not accommodate, and resolving how they should be handled is the one that constrains behaviour.

Two failure modes will present in this period, and both should be named in advance so teams can recognise them during the work.

The first failure mode is definitional inflation; the definition grows to accommodate every exception the team can think of, until it is too large to be machine-readable and too comprehensive to enforce. Scope provides the discipline. The definition describes the intended process; adaptations created under dysfunction belong elsewhere. Put workarounds for system failures in the failure-modes section and use reconstruction to decide which ones accommodate external constraints and which patch problems the unit now owns.

The second failure mode is definitional avoidance. Under pressure, a unit produces a definition that is syntactically complete but operationally vague, with states and transitions that could mean several things and invariants that remain untestable aspirations. A practical test settles the question: can a new engineer use the definition to identify the intended behaviour, the governing rules, and the precise questions that remain open? If not, the definition is not complete enough to govern implementation.

The governance charter

Process selection alone grants no authority. The governance charter distinguishes the first unit from an “empowered team” experiment by defining the unit's authority across every domain where organisations typically reclaim control through the back door.

The charter is an operational contract that specifies what the unit controls, what it excludes, and how disputes are resolved. Without it, the unit's autonomy exists only until someone challenges it, and challenge will come. Its terms make the challenge answerable.

Deployment and release form the most fundamental domain. The charter must state that the unit deploys on its own schedule within its corporate constraints and published contracts, without an additional standing approval queue. If the unit must submit ordinary changes to a change advisory board, it is performing autonomy within someone else's governance framework.

Prioritisation is where many “empowered team” experiments collapse. The charter must state that the unit decides what to work on, based on its understanding of the process, its metrics, and its contracts. Once an external body can override that prioritisation, the unit is a delivery team with a different name. The converse holds too. A unit that ignores organisational direction and treats its process as sovereign territory detached from the organisation's design has confused autonomy with independence. It prioritises within its process and contracts, against the current bets and goals that the Strategic Context Stack makes explicit.

Budget authority is the third domain. Each unit receives an allocated envelope attached to the process and decides how to spend within it. Costs are traced at the process level for visibility, but the allocation decisions within the envelope belong to the unit.

Capability composition is the fourth domain. Within law, corporate employment policy, delegated capital, and the unit's envelope, the unit decides which work is performed by employees, agents, automation, suppliers, or partners. It selects and leads its people. Employment Operations executes the employment service; institutional governance constrains and audits the exercise of authority. Neither inherits the right to design the unit by veto.

The fifth domain is contracts and inter-unit boundaries. Each unit publishes versioned, machine-readable, testable contracts defining what it provides and requires. Without explicit contracts, the unit's autonomy is undermined by invisible coupling, and this requirement is non-negotiable.

The charter must also scope centralised functions explicitly. Compliance, security, financial reporting, and brand standards retain authority over the outcomes and constraints the unit must satisfy. Where law prescribes a mechanism or the corporation operates one genuinely shared control, the mechanism also binds. Otherwise the unit owns how it complies. Compliance can require regulatory standards; security can mandate published baselines; Finance can require traceability; brand can require customer-facing consistency. None acquires general authority over process design, architecture, tooling, or release. Without these lines, central functions expand through one “reasonable requirement” at a time until the unit is governed in everything but name.

Certain functions are explicitly excluded from authority over the unit. Architecture review boards, portfolio governance committees, programme management offices, and central product management may exist elsewhere in the organisation; they have no governance role over the first unit. Any exclusion left unwritten becomes an invitation.

Dispute resolution is a separate charter clause, and it requires particular care. When the unit and another part of the organisation disagree, the default path is direct negotiation between the parties, conducted against the published contracts. If that fails, the executive sponsor facilitates a resolution; if facilitation fails, the decision escalates to the executive team. For an initial six-month period, the model proposes an asymmetric protection clause: when the dispute concerns whether the unit keeps authority already delegated by its charter, the default favours that authority. The clause never suspends law, corporate policy, risk appetite, or a valid escalation threshold. Six months is a starting design to test. Without a counterweight, every unresolved boundary dispute defaults to recentralisation and becomes a precedent for eroding the unit.

As described so far, the charter reads like a legal document, intentionally so. Its format matters as much as its content. A charter that lives only as a PDF in a SharePoint folder may be readable by AI, but its ownership, change history, references, and machine checks are difficult to maintain. The specific format matters less than three properties: engineers must be willing to maintain it, it must be version-controlled alongside the code and the contract specifications it references, and AI must be able to read it without lossy conversion. It then settles disputes by inspection: when a finance director claims approval authority over a line item, a diff against the charter replaces a meeting.

The first ninety days

This governance charter defines authority; the first ninety days test whether that authority is real.

The first quarter tests definition, operation and authority together. A process that improves under a charter that fails its first political challenge has not proved the model.

A concrete case makes the contract negotiation tangible. When an adjacent team's service writes directly to a database table the unit needs to own, the unit replaces a bare request to stop with a published contract. The unit emits events when the data changes, the adjacent team subscribes and maintains its own copy, both patterns run in parallel for a migration window, and direct database access is then revoked. A specific, versioned contract gives the conversation its ground, even where the sponsor must reinforce the unit's authority over its own data boundaries.

Some first units fail. The model depends on a well-chosen process, a capable lead, and a sponsor who holds the line. When any of these conditions fails, the organisation must name it honestly and keep it out of a vague narrative about readiness. Repeated dependence on exceptional selection or sponsorship would itself count against the model's practicality.

A retail bank charters its first unit around fraud-alert triage. The boundary looks coherent, the process is measurable, and the unit controls its software, operations, supplier, and customer messages. In four months it cuts alert-handling time and false positives without breaching its risk thresholds. The sponsor prepares to fund a second unit.

Then customer research finds a harm the scorecard missed. Older customers whose payments were stopped are abandoning digital banking after an appeal process owned by the account-servicing unit. Fraud triage met its contract: it sent the correct decision, reason code, and appeal route. Account servicing met its service level. No control failed as defined. The locally improved process made a cross-process experience worse for a group the original milestone did not name.

The sponsor pauses expansion. One group argues that the unit boundary must expand through appeal resolution. Another argues for a portfolio guardrail across both units. Risk warns that expansion could put the original decision and its appeal under one authority; Operations warns that another shared outcome will recreate a committee. The evidence establishes the harm and does not settle the design. At the next review, the unit remains operational, the expansion decision remains open, and nobody is authorised to call the first result a success.

The case is not an execution defect the method can explain away. Explicit ownership, contracts, and green local measures can optimise the wrong boundary. The model requires a human decision about which customer consequence belongs inside the unit, which belongs in a cross-unit promise, and when the autonomy benefit is outweighed by the cost of the seam. Sometimes the honest state is unresolved.

Sponsor departure before the six-month protection clause expires is one failure. The charter survives on paper while the political will behind it evaporates, and adjacent functions that were waiting for the experiment to end sense the vacuum and begin reasserting governance. The response is to find an equally committed replacement or, if none is available, to pause the experiment and say so explicitly, because a charter nobody will defend gives a unit the title of autonomy and nothing else.

Choosing the wrong process is a second failure: one too entangled to produce visible results in three months, or too peripheral to create contrast with the old model. A payments reconciliation sub-process that nobody outside finance notices is a poor vehicle for organisational change, however cleanly it decomposes. A third failure is a lead who cannot operate without the coordination structures that autonomy eliminates, who keeps scheduling alignment meetings because their absence feels like the absence of control. Both may be recoverable through a different process or lead, but the distinction must follow predeclared criteria rather than motivated interpretation. A failed unit may reveal execution failure, model failure, or both. The sponsor's final duty is to state which evidence supports each conclusion, publicly and precisely.

The organisational immune system is most active during the first three months. By then, the unit's autonomy has become visible enough to threaten adjacent power structures. The change advisory board, portfolio governance committee, and finance team each notice the unit operating outside their control, and each arrives dressed as a “concern” or a “governance gap” to be addressed. With an explicit charter and committed sponsor, every challenge begins from the document's terms and the evidence of compliance.

When the organisation pushes back

What success looks like at six months

At six months, evaluate the unit against four criteria:

  • the process definition is maintained, versioned, and reconciled against the running system;
  • contracts with adjacent units are published, versioned, and monitored;
  • leading indicators and outcomes are moving against the baseline established in week one; and
  • the unit's delegated authority has survived documented challenges without standing approvals returning.

When the evidence is ambiguous

Six-month evidence may be mixed. Resolution time improves while seasonal volume falls; experienced hires arrive during the test; contract compliance rises while customer satisfaction has not moved. The sponsor must separate a testable alternative explanation from general discomfort.

“Comparable processes improved after receiving the same hires” is a real confound. Extend the protection period, keep the charter intact, and predeclare the additional comparison. “The numbers look acceptable but I am not convinced” is not evidence. Ask what else caused the movement, then keep observing if no testable answer appears.

Leading indicators such as contract compliance, Drift reduction, and onboarding time may connect to the structural change before cost or customer outcomes do. That warrants time, without granting immunity. A sound test can be extended; contrary evidence must eventually decide it.

Do not hedge by returning priority approval to a portfolio board while leaving the unit's name in place. The intervention would change the model being tested, make its outcomes converge with the old structure, and turn temporary ambiguity into a permanent excuse.

The transition begins with a single bounded unit, a governance charter that protects accountable authority inside explicit constraints, and results that justify the next test.
