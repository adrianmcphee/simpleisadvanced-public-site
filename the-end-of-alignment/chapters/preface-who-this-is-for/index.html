Preface: Who This Is For

Why do big IT projects fail? Because they are big, and because they are IT projects.

“Big” means the company has accumulated enough fragmented authority, shared state, approvals, dependencies, and unresolved process decisions to require a programme before it can change. “IT project” means the company has then assigned that business change to a technical function it does not consider part of the business. The failure mechanism is already present in the name.

This book is for people inside the organisational form that produces both phrases: the software-dependent corporate. Its products and operations depend critically on software, but it still governs software as a support function. The quickest diagnostic is linguistic: if “the business” means a group of people that does not include engineers, and material process change becomes an “IT project”, this book is about your company and why that logic will not survive the AI era.

It is for CEOs, directors, operators, engineers, architects, product leaders, and functional executives who have watched investment produce activity without proportional outcomes and no longer believe the next reorganisation, framework, or alignment programme will repair the cause.

Corporate alignment here means the industry of recurring coordination used to compensate for fragmented authority. Shared understanding remains necessary. Permanent alignment work is the structural failure this book addresses.

The argument is deliberately ambitious. Large companies should organise authority around complete business processes, join engineering and operational expertise inside autonomous units, make promises and mechanisms readable, and use AI to reconcile what the company says with what its systems do. Governance should constrain, inspect, and challenge those units without pretending to lead them. Temporary delivery authority should be explicit all the way from a milestone to production.

What follows sets out operating logic, design constraints, and technical invariants from which a company can build its own model. No named company has implemented the complete synthesis. Public records and research support narrower mechanisms. Marked author observations describe situations I encountered directly; composites and worked examples use constructed cases and inputs. None proves that the whole design will outperform the status quo everywhere.

The evidence supports the parts more strongly than the package, which remains a testable hypothesis. Record one consequential process; give one unit the authority described here; preserve its constraints; and declare failure conditions in advance. If decision time, outcomes, risk containment, and legibility do not improve without coordination returning under another name, the design has failed that test.

Sources and Evidential Limits at the back gives the complete evidence contract, sources, technical limits, labels, and conditions that would count against the argument.

The chapters make two moves. First, they diagnose the company that alignment work keeps survivable. Then they specify the logic of an alternative from business authority down to the exact technical path through which work ships. That logic earns its place only if a company can use it to make one promise true with less delay, less ambiguity, and more inspectable accountability.
