Preface: Who This Is For

This book is for people inside a particular organisational form: the software-dependent corporate. Its products and operations depend critically on software, but it still governs software as a support function. The quickest diagnostic is linguistic: if “the business” means a group of people that does not include engineers, this book is about your company.

It is for CEOs, directors, operators, engineers, architects, product leaders, and functional executives who have watched investment produce activity without proportional outcomes and no longer believe the next reorganisation, framework, or alignment programme will repair the cause.

Corporate alignment here means the industry of recurring coordination used to compensate for fragmented authority. Shared understanding remains necessary. Permanent alignment work is the structural failure this book addresses.

The argument is deliberately ambitious. Large companies should organise authority around complete business processes, join engineering and operational expertise inside autonomous units, make promises and mechanisms readable, and use AI to reconcile what the company says with what its systems do. Governance should constrain, inspect, and challenge those units without pretending to lead them. Temporary delivery authority should be explicit all the way from a milestone to production.

What follows proposes an operating model. No named company has already implemented every part of it. The distinction matters before the argument begins. Public records establish several failure mechanisms used in these pages. Published research tests narrower relationships. Two first-person observations describe situations I directly encountered. Composite cases make recurring mechanisms concrete, but the organisations, people, sequences, and figures in them are constructed. Worked examples use invented inputs to test the method's internal logic. None of those categories proves that the complete synthesis will outperform the status quo in every company.

The public evidence therefore supports the parts more strongly than the package. Process ownership, explicit contracts, trunk-based delivery, independent challenge, and reconciled artefacts can each be examined. Their combination into one operating model remains a testable hypothesis. The strongest test is a bounded one: record the performance, risk, cost, and coordination demand of one consequential process; give one unit the authority described here; preserve its legal and institutional constraints; and predeclare what would count as failure. If elapsed decision time, outcome performance, risk containment, and legibility do not improve without coordination returning under another name, the model has failed that test.

First-person passages are marked Author observation. Composite cases are marked Composite and set in italics. Calculations using invented inputs are marked Worked example. Documented cases and published research are named. Sources and Evidential Limits at the back gives the complete evidence contract, sources, technical limits, and conditions that would count against the argument. It completes the method and should be read as such.

The chapters make two moves. First, they diagnose the company that alignment work keeps survivable. Then they specify an alternative from business authority down to the exact technical path through which work ships. The model earns its place only if a company can use it to make one promise true with less delay, less ambiguity, and more inspectable accountability.
