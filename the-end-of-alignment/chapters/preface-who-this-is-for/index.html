Preface: Who This Is For

Why do big IT projects fail? Because they are big, and because they are IT projects.

“Big” means the company has accumulated enough fragmented authority, shared state, approvals, dependencies, and unresolved process decisions to require a programme before it can change. “IT project” means the company has then assigned that business change to a technical function it does not consider part of the business. The failure mechanism is already present in the name.

This book is for people inside the organisational form that produces both phrases: the software-dependent corporate. Its products and operations depend critically on software, but it still governs software as a support function. The quickest diagnostic is linguistic: if “the business” means a group of people that does not include engineers, and material process change becomes an “IT project”, this book is about your company and why that logic will not survive the AI era.

It is for directors, leaders accountable for complete business outcomes, and the people who implement the systems those outcomes depend on. Each has watched investment produce activity without proportional results and no longer believes the next reorganisation, framework, or alignment programme will repair the cause.

Corporate alignment here means the industry of recurring coordination used to compensate for fragmented authority. Shared understanding remains necessary. Permanent alignment work is the structural failure this book addresses.

Where the logic came from

I came to this argument after more than twenty years designing, building, and leading technology inside software-dependent companies, including global platforms and engineering organisations of between 500 and 1,000 people. I was often engaged to improve an inefficient platform or technology organisation. The engineering problems were real. The binding constraints repeatedly sat around them: fragmented process authority, product and operating decisions made elsewhere, capital allocated through portfolio labels, shared data and systems, supplier choices, risk approvals, and the assumption that the engineers changing the company's operating mechanisms did not work in “the business”.

The company was paying substantially for engineering capability while withholding much of the customer, commercial, operating, and risk context required to use it well. It then paid again for product roles, OKRs, programmes, governance, and consulting to carry that context across the boundary it had created. Improving engineering alone could make one part of the system more efficient. It could not give that part authority over the process it was expected to change.

This book applies system design to that organisational problem. A company is a human institution: people have rights, judgement, history, incentives, and power, while law and sound control may deliberately keep some authorities separate. A software-dependent company also has to decide who may change operating state, where authority stops, how its parts make promises to one another, how a decision remains identifiable through implementation, and how consequences return to the people who made it. In platform design, those are questions of ownership, boundaries, contracts, provenance, and feedback. Left unresolved organisationally, the same questions appear as dependencies, recurring alignment, governance theatre, and outcomes nobody completely owns.

For the past two years I have also used the engineering practices described here in daily AI-enabled work. Before execution, I make the governing context explicit: vision and mission, goals, strategy, product definitions, milestones, architecture decisions, and requests for comment. I work backwards from an intended outcome, provide the relevant context when work is undertaken, and preserve consequential decisions in artefacts that later work can read.

This has allowed several agents to work concurrently on bounded parts of the same outcome. The gain does not come principally from asking them to think faster. It comes from resolving direction, boundaries, and important shared decisions before parallel execution begins. Each agent receives a bounded objective and the context required to pursue it; its output can be reviewed against the same milestone, decisions, and constraints. When the governing context is absent, agents produce locally coherent answers that disagree at the seams with impressive efficiency.

The governing context is a maintained design. Work exposes errors, missing decisions, and bad assumptions; the artefact changes when evidence demands it. The discipline is to make consequential thought explicit before people or agents act, then keep it available for correction as the work proceeds.

The structural problem therefore predates AI. AI changes both sides of its economics: fragmented context becomes more expensive because execution can outrun coordination, while explicit operating logic becomes more valuable because the same authoritative context can guide people and agents repeatedly.

The public cases and research in this book establish particular mechanisms and consequences. My corporate work supplies the practitioner basis from which the recurring design problem emerged. Two years of AI-native practice show the artefact-to-execution mechanism in daily use, while the Chapter 5 startup makes joined context visible at small scale. The startup is a boundary case, not a scale model of a bank. The chapters join those sources into operating logic for the corporate case: explicit authorities, coherent boundaries, binding contracts, maintained artefacts, and observable results.

The correction is operating logic derived from practice and system design, offered for testing rather than reported from completed corporate implementations.

Use whatever vocabulary your operators will maintain. If the vocabulary becomes more important than the operating tests, discard it.

Sources and Evidence closes the book with the named records, research scope, and classification of author observations, composites, worked examples, and design derivation. The chapters first diagnose the company that alignment work keeps survivable, then specify an alternative from business authority to production evidence. The result is a company better able to make a promise true with less delay, less ambiguity, and more inspectable accountability.
