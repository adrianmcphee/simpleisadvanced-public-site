Preface: Who This Is For

Why do big IT projects fail? Because they are big, and because they are IT projects.

Neither part of the phrase “big IT project” is innocent. “Big” often means that fragmented authority and shared state have made one customer or operating change large enough to require a programme. “IT project” recasts that business change as technical delivery. Process owners, budget holders, operating executives, policy makers and control functions become stakeholders. Technology receives responsibility before the work begins.

The label “IT project” also protects that allocation. If the programme fails, the company can examine delivery governance, supplier performance, architecture and execution without asking why the delivery party lacked the ordinary rights to change the process it was meant to transform. Engineering's arrival did not end the business transformation; the name merely changed who could be blamed without changing who could decide.

Some work really is a bounded technical project. Infrastructure renewal, security remediation or component replacement can have a technical purpose and a technically accountable owner. The structural diagnosis begins when work changes a customer promise, operating process, policy, control, economic mechanism or operating state. Calling that work an IT project places the failure mechanism inside the name.

This book concerns the software-dependent company: an established company whose products or operations depend on software while its authority model still treats engineering as support. If “the business” excludes engineers and material process change becomes an IT project, the description probably fits.

The conventional remedy, more alignment, is often useful at first. The right people meet, hidden dependencies become visible and decisions previously delayed are made. Yet if nobody receives authority for the complete process, each decision becomes another precedent the forum must remember. The company has increased its capacity to coordinate the fragmentation without reducing the need for coordination.

Corporate alignment here means the meetings, bridge roles, programmes, portfolio processes and consultancy used to compensate for fragmented authority. Shared understanding remains necessary. Temporary coordination remains necessary when an incident or a genuine seam crosses legitimate rights. Permanent alignment around ordinary change provides evidence about the design of the company.

A software-dependent organisation is already a distributed socio-technical system, whether or not anyone designed it to operate as one. Human and institutional actors interact through authority, software, operational data, policy, suppliers, capital and feedback. Authority therefore belongs inside the system as one of the variables that determine its behaviour. Where decision rights and state sit determines which changes can travel together and which must wait at an interface.

AI adds pressure to that socio-technical system. Whatever it makes cheaper, the question is whether the organisation can turn the extra capacity into completed outcomes. In a fragmented company, more proposals still reach separate holders of process and funding, operating data and controls, architecture and release authority. A serial permission system stays serial when proposals speed up; it produces more contradiction, review, integration work and discarded output. A process-owning unit can instead carry one complete outcome from choice through change, safe exposure, observation and recovery inside explicit institutional constraints.

Queueing theory gives that claim a boundary. Little's law links the average work inside a stable system to its throughput and the time each item spends there. Kingman's heavy-traffic result adds the operational warning: as demand approaches a fixed service capacity, waiting becomes large. Faster proposals create an infinite queue only when approval demand reaches or exceeds the rate at which the boundary can decide.

The stakes claim is a forecast, not a prophecy. AI raises what one capable person can finish without a committee, so firms whose structures charge less for change should gain ground in contested markets. An approval system that cannot absorb the extra output leaves people waiting and discards some of their work. No company is sentenced, and margins, regulation or market power can delay the comparison. If the difference affects where capable people choose to work, senior-engineer retention should reveal it early. Chapter 13 names the evidence that would make the forecast fail.

The reader I have in mind sits between the running company and the executives who can change its design. You may be a CTO, architect or operating leader; perhaps a product leader, engineer, finance partner or control specialist. You can see a mechanism that the board pack calls a delivery problem, but you cannot redraw the company by declaration. The useful move is to assemble an evidence-backed structural argument, ask for one bounded decision, and make the cost of refusal visible. Later chapters show what to hand upwards: a process boundary, a named owner, a charter, a baseline and production evidence. They also speak to the executive being asked to grant or defend authority, so you can anticipate the answer before making the request.

I came to this argument after more than twenty years of inheriting, designing and repairing organisational and software systems, including global platforms and engineering organisations of 500 to 1,000 people. Many engagements began with a request to improve Technology and exposed the binding constraints around it. Process decisions sat elsewhere, capital followed portfolio labels, shared systems resisted independent change, and engineers were expected to implement a business they were not allowed to join.

Author observation

I once explained AI with a fast train between Amsterdam and The Hague. Simple division made a train capable of 300 kilometres an hour look like a ten-minute journey. The real service takes much longer, so I blamed the power, signals and crossings. Rebuild the route, I said, and the speed becomes useful.

Experience corrected the story. I have watched companies renew pipelines, platforms and tools, then find that work still waited for signatures. Each organisational crossing had a different owner, and rebuilding the route required agreement from all of them. Faster machinery made the queue easier to see without creating one person with the right to remove it.

Executives divided the organisation by profession and budget while technical leaders divided its systems by application similarity. They then expected complete customer and operating processes to cross both sets of boundaries cheaply. Engineers could improve code and delivery inside their remit, but the system around them still determined who could decide, which state was shared and how long an ordinary process change waited. Applying system design to that larger socio-technical system is the method of this book.

A company can combine useful parts without asking every part to know everything. Each part needs the state required for its purpose and explicit interfaces at genuine seams. A department that owns one reused technical capability may still fail this test because technical similarity does not create a business boundary.

The book separates its forms of support. Research establishes component mechanisms; first-person observations identify recurring patterns from practice. Labelled composites and worked examples make those patterns inspectable without posing as documented engagements. Named public cases establish only the constraint supported by their records. A famous failure is not proof that fragmented authority caused it or that this book's correction would have prevented it.

The correction is therefore a design proposition precise enough to fail. Judge one bounded unit by five kinds of evidence: how long ordinary decisions take, how many hours recur in coordination, what production proves, what the process fully costs, and how much governance it needs to stay safe. Stop if authority does not move. Stop if local gains export greater harm, contracts cost more than the coordination they replace, or the result depends on an exceptional sponsor. Stop too if the unit cannot reproduce its account from running systems.

Chapter 4 gives the system derivation, and Chapter 13 states the first-unit test before results appear. Chapter 14 asks whether a credible result survives expansion, including the point at which new seams and central-service work cost more than the independent decisions they enable. The evidence contract stays inside the design rather than being added after the result.

Use vocabulary operators will maintain; discard any name that outranks the operating test. Sources and Evidence provides the apparatus. The separate Technical Field Guide is for readers who have won authority to administer the model.

Before the argument starts, the ask is small: charter one unit around one complete process, with declared stop conditions and a date on which the company can decide it did not work. Change nothing else until the result arrives.

Two exercises in this book need no budget or workshop. Chapter 2's meeting-language test and Chapter 15's calendar audit both produce something you can put in front of an executive by Friday.

The asymmetry between a visible, concentrated test cost and an invisible, distributed status-quo cost is the whole argument for trying. Everything after this is detail about how to try it in a way that can fail honestly.
