Preface: Who This Is For

Why do big IT projects fail? Because they are big, and because they are IT projects.

“Big” means the company has accumulated enough fragmented authority, shared state, approvals, dependencies, and unresolved process decisions to require a programme before it can change. “IT project” means the company has then assigned that business change to a technical function it does not consider part of the business. The failure mechanism is already present in the name.

This book is for people inside the organisational form that produces both phrases: the software-dependent corporate. Its products and operations depend critically on software, but it still governs software as a support function. The quickest diagnostic is linguistic. If “the business” means a group that does not include engineers, and material process change becomes an “IT project”, this book is about your company.

The primary reader is the leader trying to make a structural argument upwards: a CTO, architect, operating executive, or director who can see that the engineering problem sits inside a larger design problem. The book also speaks to the people operating the resulting systems. They have watched investment produce activity without proportional results and no longer believe the next reorganisation or alignment programme will repair the cause.

Corporate alignment here means the industry of recurring coordination used to compensate for fragmented authority. Shared understanding remains necessary. Permanent alignment work is the structural failure this book addresses.

I came to this argument after inheriting, designing, building, and repairing more complex organisational and software systems than I can now reliably count, across more than twenty years inside software-dependent companies. That work included global platforms and engineering organisations of between 500 and 1,000 people. I was often engaged to improve an inefficient platform or technology organisation. The engineering problems were real. The binding constraints repeatedly sat around them: fragmented process authority, product and operating decisions made elsewhere, capital allocated through portfolio labels, shared data and systems, supplier choices, risk approvals, and the assumption that the engineers changing the company's operating mechanisms did not work in “the business”.

The company was paying substantially for engineering capability while withholding much of the customer, commercial, operating, and risk context required to use it well. It then paid again for product roles, OKRs, programmes, governance, and consulting to carry context across the boundary it had created. Improving engineering alone could make one part of the system more efficient. It could not give that part authority over the process it was expected to change.

A software-dependent organisation is already a distributed socio-technical system, whether or not its operating model recognises the fact. Once data and AI act across it, that fact becomes operationally unavoidable. Software, data, and agents change the running company between meetings while continuous alignment reconciles partial representations at calendar speed. Its design still has to locate state, authority, interfaces, identity, provenance, and feedback. Left unresolved, they reappear as dependencies, governance theatre, and outcomes nobody completely owns.

Law, employment rights and controls such as separation of duties sometimes require authority to be split. Good design names the protected right and the harm it prevents. It does not turn every legitimate constraint into a standing invitation to manage somebody else's process.

For the past two years I have also used the engineering practices described here in daily AI-enabled work. I make direction, product intent, milestones, architecture decisions, and unresolved questions explicit before execution. That preparation allows several agents to work concurrently on bounded parts of one outcome. When the governing context is absent, they produce locally coherent answers that disagree at the seams with impressive efficiency.

The structural problem predates AI. This is the reason for publishing now. Companies that use alignment to keep shared state, ambiguous boundaries, and fragmented authority survivable are increasing the rate of execution across all three. Fragmented context becomes more expensive when execution outruns coordination; explicit operating logic becomes more valuable because people and agents can read it repeatedly.

The evidence has several forms and each is labelled. Named public cases and research establish specific failure mechanisms. First-person observations come from my practice. Composites and worked examples make a mechanism inspectable without pretending to be documented engagements. The correction is a design derivation: established system constraints applied to organisational authority, then modified for law, rights, judgement, incentives, and power. It is offered for testing rather than reported from completed corporate implementations. The test is whether decision time, coordination load, production evidence, process economics, and governance overhead improve against the process's own baseline.

Use whatever vocabulary your operators will maintain. If the vocabulary becomes more important than the operating tests, discard it.

Sources and Evidence closes the book with the full apparatus. The chapters first diagnose the company that alignment work keeps survivable, then derive an alternative from business authority to production evidence. The worked packet shows what one implementation might look like. The argument begins here.
