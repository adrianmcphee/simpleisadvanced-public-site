Abolish Central People Strategy

The technical model makes temporary execution authority explicit. The same unit must decide what enduring capability performs the process: people, agents, automation, suppliers, or a combination. Organisation design belongs with the leader who owns the outcome.

The leader owns a revenue line, customer promise, regulated process, cost base, and operational result. Yet a central People function defines job families, organisation principles, workforce planning, leadership models, succession methods, learning architecture, and a company-wide account of what capability means. The leader must execute through an organisation someone else has designed while remaining accountable when it cannot perform.

That arrangement was incoherent before agents. Agents make it absurd.

Engineering, Finance, Marketing, Logistics, Sales, and Operations do not share one capability strategy. Their work, labour markets, failure modes, professional communities, technology curves, location constraints, and rates of automation differ. Twenty-five finance specialists and five hundred engineers should not share a community model, career architecture, learning system, or location logic merely because all have employment contracts.

No credible central “people strategy” can decide across those domains. Process-owning executives must decide which capabilities they need and how to obtain them.

Abolition is literal about authority and deliberately narrow about function. The central function loses authority to design other executives' organisations, define their capability strategies, or approve how they compose people, agents, software, suppliers, and partners. The corporation still employs people and must exercise authority lawfully, so bounded employment services, institutional governance, professional advice, and activities with a real business owner remain. Work that cannot identify one of those relationships should be retired. Moving it intact under a new chief would preserve the category error.

The case for abolition rests on the allocation logic and the bounded test in Chapter 12. The author observation later in this chapter illustrates one weak artefact and carries no wider claim.

Capability is broader than headcount

For a process owner, hiring is one option among several. Work can be performed by employees, agents, deterministic automation, software products, contractors, outsourcers, platforms, suppliers, partners, or a deliberate combination. The design question is not “How many people do you need?” It is “What capability must exist for this process to deliver its promise, manage its risk, and recover when something fails?”

That decision includes organisation design, staffing, skills, location, leadership, performance, development, and succession. It also includes what should be insourced, outsourced, automated, purchased, or obtained through partnership. Splitting those choices across HR, Procurement, Technology, Finance, and Operations fragments a single capability design into several approval processes owned by people who do not own the outcome.

Agents sharpen the point. A unit may discover that one part of a process is best performed by a small group of experienced domain people with agent assistance; another by deterministic software; another by a regulated supplier; another by an employee because judgement and institutional accountability must remain inside. The mix may change every quarter. A central workforce plan cannot sensibly prescribe it without understanding the process, its economics, its controls, and the current performance of the available mechanisms.

Business leaders therefore own their organisations and capabilities. That is not an argument for arbitrary management. Their authority is bounded by law, corporate policy, delegated capital, risk appetite, worker protections, contractual commitments, and audit. Inside those constraints, accountability must be joined to the ability to compose the means of performance.

Apply the same capability test to Finance

The same organisation that expects AI to reduce engineering headcount can treat the current shape of Finance as fixed. The asymmetry does not survive inspection. It treats software production as typing and recurring financial production as judgement, although a large share of management reporting and financial planning is structurally well suited to AI and deterministic automation.

Much of management reporting and financial planning and analysis describes lagging indicators after a process has acted. Reconciliations, management-pack production, recurring variance explanations, forecast updates, control evidence assembly, and first-pass analysis repeat acts of reading, comparing, classifying, calculating, and narrating over structured inputs and recurring calendars. A capable analytics team, working close to operating units and using AI, can produce much of that traceable synthesis and connect financial results with process behaviour.

The engineer who understands why a twenty-year-old operational system behaves differently from its architecture diagram is a harder person to replace. The work is not mainly producing syntax. It involves reconstructing missing intent, testing real behaviour, designing boundaries, making trade-offs under partial evidence, and changing a live mechanism without breaking the process it performs. AI makes a strong engineer dramatically more capable. It does not make the running company intelligible by itself.

Finance does not disappear. Statutory accounting, tax, treasury, capital allocation, fiduciary judgement, accounting policy, challenge, controls, and accountability remain serious work. Finance must apply the same capability logic to itself that it applies elsewhere. A CFO should be free to create high-judgement roles, automate routine analysis, and shrink the reporting factory. A CEO can do the same when redesigning the executive system; neither needs a central People function to invent roles or grant permission.

This model raises the standard of financial understanding across the company. Financial understanding is weak across many companies, including among leaders who control material budgets. The organisation has trained them to submit numbers to Finance and receive an interpretation back rather than understand the economic system they operate.

Finance should treat financial literacy across the company as an operating responsibility. Accepting widespread financial illiteracy as a permanent fact leaves process owners unable to understand the economics they control. A short, concentrated introduction can give a capable person enough grounding in profit and loss, cash flow, balance-sheet movement, unit economics, variance, and investment analysis to ask intelligent questions. Practice then deepens that understanding. Accounting expertise remains a profession; process owners still need to understand the economics they control.

AI lowers the cost of that education. It can explain the company's own statements in context, interrogate assumptions, trace operational movements into financial effects, compare scenarios, and let a capable person explore an investment case without waiting for a reporting cycle. Process owners, operators, product leaders, and engineers should understand cash flow, margins, unit economics, capital consumption, accounting consequences, forecast uncertainty, and the difference between cost and value. Finance specialists retain authoritative standards and independent challenge without becoming the required interpreter of every economic fact.

Financial literacy inside autonomous units closes another destructive gap: the people deciding how the process works can see what it costs and what it produces. They can make trade-offs before the result appears months later as a lagging indicator in a central pack. More leaders who understand the economics make the company easier for the CFO to govern, while Finance concentrates on work that requires its judgement.

Every executive owns this question inside their own boundary: which human judgement is genuinely scarce, which work is codifiable, which work can be delegated to agents, and which existing role survives only because the process has never been redesigned?

Put employment operations where they belong

Abolishing a separate People strategy does not make payroll, benefits, employee records, immigration administration, onboarding logistics, case management, or employment advice disappear. These are real operational services. They should be run well, measured for accuracy and service, and purchased where purchase is better than ownership.

Call the service People Operations, Employment Operations, or simply Employment Services. Give it explicit service promises, interfaces, controls, and economics. It supports the process-owning leaders who make organisation decisions. It maintains authoritative employment records and executes transactions. It does not turn administration into a claim of enterprise leadership.

The modern People function often redescribes services as strategic architecture. The result can be a vocabulary factory: job-family frameworks, capability taxonomies, talent grids, belonging maturity models, leadership potential matrices, career architectures, ambassador networks, listening strategies, and councils to govern the councils. The underlying concerns may be legitimate. The operational specification needed for a decision can still be missing.

At one employer, I asked for the actual job specifications because I wanted to compare proposed roles with the ones that already existed. I received something closer to a children's storybook than a job specification. It described careers without specifying the work precisely enough to make the comparison. The problem was not taste. A business was trying to determine whether two jobs were materially different, and the artefact could not support the decision.

The test for any people artefact is the same test applied elsewhere in this book: what decision does it enable, what operating reality does it describe, who owns the meaning, and what evidence could prove it wrong? If the answer is sentiment, consistency, or engagement without a mechanism connecting it to the work, the artefact is decoration.

Professional communities belong near the profession

Community, learning, mentoring, and professional identity matter. Centralising them is not the only way to take them seriously.

The finance community may need a precise technical curriculum, rotations across a small number of specialist roles, professional accreditation, and a close succession view because only twenty-five people hold the capability. An engineering population of five hundred may need many technical communities, internal open-source practices, architecture forums tied to real decisions, apprenticeship, incident learning, and several distinct leadership paths. Sales may need field coaching organised around markets. Operations may need shift-based learning and local practice communities. Treating all of these as one employee experience produces generic programmes that fit none of them.

The process-owning executives and professional leaders should build the communities their work needs. Some services can be shared: event logistics, platforms, venues, facilitation, and administration. The content, membership, cadence, and purpose belong to the capability owners. A summer party can be company-wide. A community of practice cannot be designed as if the practice were incidental.

Create one institutional governance executive

Removing the strategic pretensions of HR leaves an important design question: where do the functions that govern the corporation's exercise of authority belong?

Legal, Compliance, Procurement, Public Affairs, Communications, employee relations, and appropriate Risk and Control functions should sit under one senior executive. Employment Operations can sit there too. These functions govern how the corporation contracts, employs, buys, communicates, deals with regulators, sets institutional standards, and manages legal, reputational, conduct, supplier, and public risk.

The role requires an unusually strong person: legally literate, commercially intelligent, trusted under pressure, good with people, and an exceptional communicator. In many companies the best candidate will be a first-rate general counsel whose judgement extends well beyond legal interpretation. The mandate is to make institutional constraints coherent and quick to apply, support business owners who actually run the processes, and avoid building a larger central empire.

The exact title matters less than the boundary. “Chief Institutional Officer” is one possibility, although companies already crowded with chiefs should resist using a title to avoid specifying authority. The charter should say which policies this executive owns, which matters require independent challenge, what thresholds trigger escalation, which operational services are provided, and what decisions remain with process leaders.

This grouping reflects a common function: these teams govern the institution's exercise of authority. They do not share a right to lead every business process they touch.

This grouping leaves People strategy behind. Organisation design, staffing, leadership, succession, capability composition, and professional development have moved to the executives who own the relevant processes. The institutional executive receives no general mandate over the workforce. The centre is narrower in strategic authority even if several governance and service teams share one reporting line.

The grouping does not erase legally required independence or direct access to the board and its committees. An internal-audit leader, regulated compliance officer, or risk executive may need protected reporting rights that no institutional executive can filter. The design unifies support and institutional policy where coherent; it does not use an org chart to override law, regulation, professional duty, or independent assurance.

Governance is not leadership

Leadership is prospective. It chooses direction, designs the process, composes capability, takes action, and owns the outcome.

Governance defines delegated authority and constraints, observes evidence, challenges exposure, and acts when thresholds are crossed. Audit independently tests whether processes, controls, and representations operate as declared. Governance and audit are essential. Neither is a substitute for leadership.

The distinction often blurs when a central function can gain influence without accepting outcome accountability. A governance team issues a framework, requires an approval, joins a steering group, and gradually becomes shadow management. When the process succeeds, the operating unit delivered it. When it fails, the unit remains accountable. The centre had a veto without ownership.

Autonomous units must be governed and audited. They handle customers, money, personal data, regulated obligations, suppliers, and increasingly powerful agents. Their process definitions, decisions, controls, contracts, model use, production identity, incidents, economics, and outcomes must be inspectable. Their authority exists inside explicit corporate and legal constraints. None of that gives a central function the right to make ordinary operating decisions it cannot competently make.

The rule is simple: governance can constrain, challenge, inspect, and escalate. It cannot pretend to lead a process whose result it does not own.

Regulated autonomy clarifies accountability

Banks, insurers, and other regulated entities cannot use autonomy to dissolve individual accountability or protected control functions. Nor does this model ask them to. Local law and regulatory allocation prevail. The operating model maps delegated process authority beneath those duties and makes the evidence of their exercise inspectable.

The UK's Senior Managers and Certification Regime requires clear statements of responsibility and overall senior-manager responsibility across the firm's activities. A process-owning unit strengthens that arrangement when its charter, controls, suppliers, models, production state, and outcomes trace to the accountable senior manager. It gives that person better evidence of reasonable steps than a committee in which everyone attended and no one owned the mechanism.

The same structure answers model-risk expectations. The unit owns the model's intended use inside its process, the data and decisions it affects, human override, operating performance, limitations, change behaviour, incidents, and exit path. A specialist second line sets classification and policy, independently validates or challenges according to materiality, aggregates exposure, and can require remediation or restrict use when thresholds are crossed. The board and senior management retain the responsibilities regulation assigns to them. Independent audit tests the framework and its operation.

The EU Digital Operational Resilience Act is equally direct: the management body retains ultimate responsibility for ICT risk and must oversee third-party arrangements. An autonomous unit therefore cannot relabel a critical provider as an IT dependency and send accountability elsewhere. It owns how the provider participates in its process, while the management body and control functions retain their institutional duties.

Governance must bite here. A second line that cannot obtain evidence, require remediation, escalate a breach, or stop activity at an authorised threshold is decorative. A second line that designs and co-runs every first-line process has crossed into leadership without accepting the outcome. Regulated autonomy keeps both failures visible.

Policy is not a control

The centre has a legitimate policy role. A policy states a corporate obligation or constraint: payments above a threshold require separation of duties; customer data may be used only for specified purposes; material suppliers require continuity evidence; high-impact automated decisions require traceability and appeal.

A control is how a particular process satisfies the policy. It sits in the states, decisions, systems, roles, evidence, and failure behaviour of that process.

A central function that does not understand the process cannot design the control reliably. It can define the outcome the control must achieve, provide expertise, challenge the design, test operation, and reject evidence that fails the standard. The process owner designs and operates the control because the process owner understands where the risk enters and what mechanism can contain it.

Confusing policy with control creates paperwork compliance. Units complete the same template, install the same tool, or add the same approval even when the mechanism does not address their risk. Dashboards show adoption while the exposure survives untouched. The centre measures whether units consumed what it pushed rather than whether they manage the risk they own.

Good governance therefore operates as three joined lines without turning them into three management hierarchies. The process-owning unit is the first line: it owns risk and operates controls. Institutional governance and specialist risk provide second-line constraints, advice, monitoring, and challenge. Independent audit provides third-line assurance to the governing body. The second and third lines do not inherit the first line's process ownership.

Outsourcing never outsources ownership

The same distinction corrects third-party risk management. Companies often treat a supplier reached through an API as an IT dependency. The issue goes to technology risk because there is a technical integration, while the business sourcing decision that placed part of a customer process outside the company disappears from view.

The integration is only the mechanism. If a provider performs identity checks, prices a transaction, stores a record, makes a decision, communicates with a customer, or moves money, it performs part of the business process. The owning unit remains accountable for the promise, the control design, monitoring, continuity, recovery, data use, subcontractors, concentration, and exit path. A contract may allocate obligations. Indemnity and insurance may allocate financial loss. None transfers the company's ownership of whether its process works.

AI providers make this boundary more urgent. The unit must know which decisions an agent may make, which data it may access, where human authority remains, how behaviour is evaluated, what happens when a model or provider changes, how incidents are detected, and how the process continues if the service disappears. Procurement can negotiate terms. Legal can define protections. Risk can challenge concentration and control evidence. Technology can assess the integration. The unit owns the decision to use the capability inside its process and the consequences of doing so.

Central governance should aggregate institutional exposure across units: concentration in one provider, repeated control weakness, legal inconsistency, systemic model risk, or supplier chains no unit can see alone. It should set thresholds and require correction. It should not convert aggregate visibility into ownership of each process that uses a supplier.

The organisation becomes designable again

Once capability authority sits with process owners, organisation design becomes part of operating design. Leaders can compare labour, agents, automation, suppliers, and partnerships against the same outcome, economics, risk, and recovery requirements. They can build professional communities that fit the work. They can change the mix as evidence changes. Employment Operations can become excellent at service. Institutional governance can become excellent at protecting the corporation without impersonating its leaders.

The aim is to end a category error. Headcount follows from the work that remains. People participate in capabilities designed to perform real processes, alongside technology, suppliers, capital, and increasingly agents; they are not a separate strategy layered across the businesses that employ them.

An executive who owns a process owns the organisation required to run it. If that authority is missing, the accountability is theatre. If it is present without governance, the autonomy is reckless. The AI-era operating model requires both, and it requires everyone to know the difference.
