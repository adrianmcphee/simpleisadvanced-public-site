The Alignment-Industrial Complex

How Fragmented Authority Destroys a Company's Ability to Compete

THE MEETINGS WENT WELL.

Every week, 250 customers call this insurer to correct their mileage. Letting them do it online would save customers time and the insurer money. Customers want it, owners benefit, and management agrees it matters. Two years later, the calls are still coming.

For executives, operators, product leaders, engineers and boards.

Every department can meet its targets.

At the fictional insurer in this book, every department can meet its targets while the company fails to deliver. Agreement changes none of the permissions required to finish. Keeping the departments aligned becomes a business of its own, with budgets and careers to protect.

While the company negotiates permission, a competitor can improve its service and learn from the result. Giving someone the authority to finish means taking power from people the company is already rewarding. Those people help decide what changes. The agreed improvement is better alignment.

Inside the company

The work waits

An ordinary correction becomes a programme. Every function can explain its part. The customer still cannot change the number.

The reports improve

A board receives accurate reports about a company it cannot inspect. People closest to the work know what the account leaves out.

Someone has to give up power

Moving authority makes ordinary improvement possible. It also exposes failed boundaries, duplicated costs and work that must stop.

The result

The customer is still waiting.
Two years of bonuses have been paid in full.
